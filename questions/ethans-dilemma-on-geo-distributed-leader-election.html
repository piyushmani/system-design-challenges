Ethan's Dilemma on Geo-Distributed Leader Election

Ethan owns a metadata service for a globally distributed key-value system running in three regions (us-east, eu-west, ap-south). The service holds compact, hot data (shard mappings, ID allocation sequences and lease information) and must sustain tens of thousands of metadata operations per second with low read latency for local clients. Leases and unique-ID allocation require strict (linearizable) consistency, and regional network partitions are expected. Most keys have a clear home region where the bulk of their traffic originates. He must choose an architecture for leader election and coordination that balances scalability, multi-region availability, regional read latency, strict consistency and observability during leader changes.

A
A single global Raft cluster with voters in every region; one leader serves all metadata, and cross-region reads are forwarded to it to guarantee linearizability.
✗ Not Best
B
A separate ZooKeeper ensemble per region, asynchronously replicated across regions, with failover that promotes a leader from another region when a regional leader fails.
✗ Not Best
C
Shard the metadata into independent Raft groups, each electing its own leader placed in the region that generates most of its traffic; route clients to the right shard leader and use asynchronous cross-region replication or snapshots for disaster recovery.
✓ Best Answer
D
A leaderless design using CRDTs or eventual consistency, where any node accepts local writes and conflicts are resolved deterministically in background merges.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: Sharded Raft Groups

Given these requirements (linearizable leases and ID allocation, tens of thousands of operations per second, expected regional partitions, strong locality of access), per-shard Raft groups with leaders placed near their traffic is the most appropriate strategy. It keeps the consensus protocol that gives you correctness, but removes the two things that break a single global cluster: one leader as a throughput bottleneck, and a cross-region round trip on every operation. Here's why it stands out:

  • Correctness where it matters: Each shard is a Raft group, so lease grants and ID-block allocations are linearizable, and every leader change bumps a monotonic term. That term (or the log index) doubles as a fencing token, so a deposed lease holder cannot keep acting on stale authority.
  • Locality of latency: A leader placed in the shard's home region serves linearizable reads locally (leader lease or ReadIndex-style reads), so local clients avoid a cross-ocean round trip. Write latency is bounded by the quorum placement you choose per shard, rather than by the farthest region.
  • Scalability and a smaller blast radius: Throughput scales with the number of shards because each group has its own leader, log and election. A leader failure or partition only forces re-election in the shards it led; the rest of the keyspace keeps serving. Hot shards can be rebalanced by transferring leadership or splitting.
  • Observability: Every group exposes the same signals (current leader, term, election count, commit latency, replication lag, lease-expiry margin), so leader shifts become first-class, per-shard events that can be alerted on and correlated with client errors.
  • Proven at scale: Google Spanner (Paxos groups per split), CockroachDB (a Raft group per range with a leaseholder) and TiKV (multi-Raft regions) all use per-shard consensus with locality-aware leader placement.

Treat consensus as a per-shard primitive, not a global singleton: use locality-aware leader placement, fencing tokens on every lease, ID-block allocation so clients hand out IDs locally, and leader-change observability (term, election count, commit latency). Rehearse regional failure with partition drills (Jepsen-style or chaos tests) before relying on it.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🌐

A — Single Global Raft Cluster

One group gives simple, strong semantics, but every write pays a cross-region quorum round trip and a single leader must handle all tens of thousands of operations per second. Linearizable reads from the two non-leader regions have to be forwarded to (or confirmed with) the leader, so 'low local read latency' is lost for most clients. A leader loss, or a partition that isolates the leader's region, stalls the whole metadata plane at once. Caveat: for a small, low-write control plane (hundreds of operations per second), a single global group, like one etcd cluster, is perfectly reasonable and far simpler to operate.

🗄️

B — Per-Region ZooKeeper + Async Replication

ZooKeeper ensembles are excellent inside a region, but linking them with asynchronous replication means two regions can each believe they hold a lease or have allocated the same ID range during a partition (split brain), and promoting a remote leader loses any writes that were acknowledged but not yet replicated. Failover becomes a scripted or manual procedure rather than a protocol guarantee, which fails the 'strict consistency for leases and allocations' requirement. Caveat: ZooKeeper observers give cheap local reads, but writes still funnel through one leader, so a single stretched ensemble brings back option A's latency.

🔀

D — Leaderless CRDTs / Eventual Consistency

CRDTs give excellent availability and local latency, but mutual exclusion and uniqueness cannot be achieved without coordination. Two regions accepting concurrent local writes can both grant the same lease or hand out the same ID, and a deterministic merge can only decide who 'wins' after both have acted. Caveat: CRDTs fit data that tolerates convergence (counters, presence, caches), and they work for IDs when each region owns a disjoint range handed out by a consensus-backed allocator.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Single Global Raft

Low write rate (hundreds of operations per second), a small dataset, and simplicity valued above regional read latency, such as a global control plane or configuration store.

📌

B — ZooKeeper per Region

Single-region coordination (service discovery, locks, configuration) where ZooKeeper's mature recipes fit, or read-only remote copies via observers. Not for cross-region mutual exclusion.

📌

C — Sharded Raft Groups

High-throughput metadata with regional locality of access, strict leases and allocations, and expected partitions. This is the pattern behind Spanner, CockroachDB and TiKV.

📌

D — Leaderless CRDT

Data that tolerates convergence (presence, counters, caches, collaborative state), or ID spaces pre-partitioned by a consensus-backed allocator.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Treating asynchronous DR as zero-loss: If voters live only in the home region, promoting a remote copy after a regional outage can lose acknowledged writes. Document the RPO, and keep strict shards on cross-region quorums.
  • Leases without fencing: A paused or partitioned client (long GC pause, VM stall) can keep acting on an expired lease. Always carry a fencing token (term and index) and make the protected resource reject lower tokens.
  • Election timeouts below the WAN round trip: Followers time out before heartbeats arrive, causing constant elections. Use timeouts several times the worst-case RTT plus jitter, and enable Pre-Vote and CheckQuorum.
  • Serving follower reads as if they were linearizable: Follower reads can be stale. Use leader-lease or ReadIndex reads for lease and allocator state, and follower reads only where bounded staleness is acceptable.
  • Too many tiny Raft groups: Each group has heartbeats, timers and a log, so thousands of groups multiply background traffic. Use multi-Raft batching (coalesced heartbeats) and size shards for load.
  • No leader-change telemetry: Without term, election count and reason, a 30-second blip stays invisible until clients time out. Instrument every transition.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, teams treat this as a placement-and-consistency policy problem, not just an algorithm choice. A typical sequence looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Classify the metadata: Separate data that needs linearizability (leases, ID allocators, shard ownership) from data that tolerates staleness (cached shard maps, read-mostly configuration). Each class gets its own replication and read policy.
  • Step 1 — Shard into Raft groups: Partition by key range or hash into many small groups, starting with enough shards that leaders can be spread across all three regions.
  • Step 2 — Place leaders and voters by locality: Apply a placement policy (leader preference in the home region; three voters across regions for critical shards, or three voters in-region plus asynchronous DR for loss-tolerant ones) and automate leader transfer when traffic shifts.
  • Step 3 — Add fencing and local fast paths: Issue fencing tokens from the term and log index with every lease, allocate IDs in blocks (for example 1,000 at a time) so most allocations never touch the leader, and use leader-lease reads for local linearizable reads.
  • Step 4 — Make elections observable: Export term, leader identity, election count, commit and apply latency, lease-expiry margin and follower lag per shard, and log every leadership change with its reason. Alert on elections per minute and on shards without a leader.
  • Step 5 — Test regional failure: Run partition and region-loss drills, and measure time-to-new-leader and the write-unavailability window against your SLO.

Pick replica placement per shard class, and let measured failover behavior, not assumptions, decide where leaders live.

💡

Key Takeaway

Don't use one global leader, and don't give up consistency. Run many independent consensus groups, put each leader next to its traffic, and fence every lease with the Raft term. Choose replica placement per shard to trade write latency against regional disaster survival, and measure elections and failover time continuously.
