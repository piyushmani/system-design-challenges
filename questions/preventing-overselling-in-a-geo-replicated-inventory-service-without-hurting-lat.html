Challenge

Preventing Overselling in a Geo-Replicated Inventory Service Without Hurting Latency

Aiden owns the inventory microservice of a worldwide e-commerce site. It must never allow negative stock (no overselling), give shoppers in every region low-latency reads, and stay available when a whole region fails. Orders (writes) are frequent and can originate anywhere, but the stock of a given SKU is decided by a single logical counter, traffic is reasonably spread across SKUs, and the business accepts that a product page may show slightly stale availability as long as the final checkout decision is always correct. He needs the most balanced, observable and maintainable pattern.

A
Globally synchronous consensus (multi-region Raft/Paxos or a Spanner-like system) that replicates every write to all regions before acknowledging. Globally correct, but write latency is high and availability drops during partitions.
✗ Not Best
B
Asynchronous primary-replica replication with one global primary handling all writes and read replicas in other regions. Fast local reads through eventual consistency, with conflicts resolved by last-write-wins (LWW).
✗ Not Best
C
Partition inventory by SKU with one authoritative leader per partition and a local consensus group (Raft/Paxos) replicating writes synchronously to nearby failure domains and asynchronously to distant regions for local reads. Route writes to the leader, use CAS/versioning for critical updates, and use read-your-writes routing to avoid stale reads.
✓ Best Answer
D
Active-active multi-master replication across all regions using CRDTs or custom conflict resolution. Every region accepts writes independently and converges eventually through tuned merge rules.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: SKU-Partitioned Leaders

The invariant stock ≥ 0 is a single-key constraint, so it needs one serialization point per SKU, not one global serialization point. Partitioning by SKU gives each item its own leader and consensus group: writes for different SKUs proceed in parallel, correctness is enforced locally by compare-and-set, and distant regions get fast reads from asynchronous copies. Here's why it stands out:

  • Correctness where it counts: Every decrement for a SKU goes through that SKU's leader as a conditional update (stock >= qty), so concurrent orders are serialized and the counter can never go negative.
  • Low write latency: Consensus runs among nearby replicas (for example three zones or metro-close sites), so a write commits in a few milliseconds instead of waiting for the farthest region. SKUs are homed near their main demand.
  • Fast reads everywhere: Remote regions hold non-voting replicas updated asynchronously, so product pages read locally. Read-your-writes routing sends a shopper who just ordered to a replica that has applied their write, or to the leader.
  • Region-failure resilience: Voting replicas span failure domains, so losing a zone or the leader's region leaves a quorum that elects a new leader without losing acknowledged decrements. Other SKUs are unaffected.
  • Observable and maintainable: Per-partition metrics (leader location, commit latency, replica lag, rejection rate) pinpoint hot or unhealthy SKUs, and partitions can be moved or split independently.

A strong implementation includes idempotency keys on every order, conditional (CAS) decrements, reservations with expiry for carts, read-your-writes tokens, leader placement near demand, and dashboards for oversell rejections, replica lag and leader elections. Treat 'rejected for insufficient stock' as a normal business outcome and monitor its rate.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🌍

A — Global Synchronous Consensus

Provides the strongest global guarantees, but waiting for every region on each write puts the slowest, farthest link in the critical path, and requiring all regions (rather than a majority) to acknowledge turns any regional outage or partition into a write outage. Even a majority quorum across continents costs tens to hundreds of milliseconds per order. It buys cross-SKU consistency that this problem does not need. Caveat: appropriate for small, truly global metadata (uniqueness constraints, global balances) where latency is secondary.

🏛️

B — Single Primary + Async Replicas + LWW

Easy to run, but every order pays a trip to one global primary, that primary's region is a single point of failure, and async replicas serve stale stock during lag. Last-write-wins is actively harmful for counters: two concurrent decrements overwrite each other, so inventory is wrongly restored, which causes overselling. Caveat: primary-replica with synchronous failover is fine for regional services with modest write volume; LWW suits idempotent overwrites, not arithmetic.

🔀

D — Active-Active CRDTs

Plain counter CRDTs converge, but they cannot enforce a lower bound: two regions can each sell the last unit, and the merged value goes negative. Bounded-counter or escrow variants avoid this by pre-allocating stock shares to each region, but that is partitioned authority with rebalancing complexity, not 'tuned merge rules'. Caveat: a good fit for non-critical counters (views, likes) or as an escrow layer for extremely hot SKUs.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Global Synchronous Consensus

Small, globally shared, correctness-critical state with low write rates (account uniqueness, global balances) where latency is a secondary concern.

📌

B — Single Primary + Async Replicas

Mostly regional workloads with modest writes, simple operations, and overwrite-style data where lost updates are tolerable.

📌

C — SKU-Partitioned Leaders

High write rates across many independent keys with a hard invariant per key, global reads and region-failure tolerance.

📌

D — Active-Active CRDTs

Commutative, non-critical data (likes, views, presence) or escrow-style counters for extremely hot keys.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Approving orders from replica reads: A stale replica says 3 are left when 0 remain. Only the leader's conditional update may approve an order.
  • Read-then-write decrements: Reading stock, computing, then writing back loses updates under concurrency. Use UPDATE ... SET stock = stock - ? WHERE stock >= ? or CAS.
  • Promoting an async replica after failure: It may be missing acknowledged orders. Promote only members of the synchronous voting set.
  • No idempotency keys: Client or queue retries decrement twice. Store the order ID with the decrement in the same transaction.
  • Ignoring hot SKUs: One viral item can saturate a single leader. Plan buckets or reservation queues before the sale.
  • Forgetting reservation expiry: Abandoned carts that never release stock cause phantom sell-outs. Use TTL-based reservations.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, inventory systems separate authoritative decisions from cheap approximate reads, and they measure oversell and rejection rates. A typical sequence looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Define the invariant and staleness budget: Stock is never negative, displayed availability may lag by seconds, and cart reservations hold stock for a bounded time.
  • Step 1 — Partition by SKU: Give each SKU an authoritative leader and a small voting group across failure domains near its main demand.
  • Step 2 — Conditional decrements: Use single-statement conditional updates or CAS with versions, plus idempotency keys, for every order and cancellation.
  • Step 3 — Regional read replicas: Serve product pages from local non-voting replicas, with read-your-writes routing for shoppers who just changed stock.
  • Step 4 — Hot SKU strategy: Split hot SKUs into stock buckets or add a reservation queue for flash sales, keeping the final decision atomic.
  • Step 5 — Failure drills and reconciliation: Test leader loss and region isolation, and run periodic reconciliation between orders and stock to catch drift.

Serialize per SKU, replicate for reads, and keep the final decision with the leader.

💡

Key Takeaway

Overselling is a per-SKU serialization problem, not a global-consensus problem. Give each SKU one authoritative leader backed by a nearby consensus group, replicate asynchronously for local reads, and always make the final stock decision with a conditional update at the leader.
