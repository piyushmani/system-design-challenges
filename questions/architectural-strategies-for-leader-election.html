Architectural Strategies for Leader Election

Sarah, a software engineer, is building a distributed system in which leader election is necessary for high availability. The cluster spans several nodes (and availability zones), crashes and network partitions will happen, and a split brain (two nodes both acting as leader) would corrupt data. The leader should be reachable by the other nodes with the lowest possible latency, and failover after a leader failure must be fast and automatic. She needs to choose the best election approach.

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — B: Raft Consensus with a Leader Lease

Leader election is a consensus problem: the cluster must agree on at most one leader per term even with crashes and partitions. Raft solves this with majority voting, terms and randomized election timeouts, so there is never more than one legitimate leader at a time. A leader lease adds a time-bounded promise that no rival leader will be elected until the lease expires. That lets the leader serve reads from local state without a quorum round trip and keeps leadership stable. Here's why it stands out:

  • Safety first: A candidate needs votes from a majority, so a partitioned minority can never elect a rival leader. That rules out split brain.
  • Low steady-state latency: Heartbeats are cheap, and with a valid lease the leader answers reads from local state instead of contacting a quorum each time. CockroachDB's range leases and TiKV's lease reads use this idea.
  • Fast, automatic failover: Randomized election timeouts (typically hundreds of milliseconds, scaled to network round-trip time) allow a new leader to emerge within about a second or a few. Leadership transfer can move the leader close to its clients.
  • Built-in fencing: The term number is a monotonically increasing fencing token. Downstream resources can reject writes carrying an older term, which neutralizes stale leaders.
  • Mature implementations: etcd, Consul and TiKV use Raft, and Kubernetes relies on etcd (Raft) plus Lease objects for component leader election. The algorithm comes from Ongaro and Ousterhout (USENIX ATC 2014).

Run 3 or 5 voting nodes, enable pre-vote and check-quorum to avoid disruptive elections, set election timeouts relative to measured round-trip time (etcd's guidance is at least ten times the round trip), use the term as a fencing token, and prefer an existing implementation such as etcd or Consul over building your own.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🦓

A — ZooKeeper with Synchronous Communication

ZooKeeper is a proven coordination service (ephemeral nodes and watches make election straightforward, and it uses its own Zab protocol rather than Raft), so this is a valid choice. But it means running and depending on a separate coordination cluster, with extra round trips, session-timeout-based failure detection (seconds) and client code that must handle session expiry correctly. The emphasis on synchronous communication adds latency rather than removing it. Caveat: if the organization already runs ZooKeeper, it is a reasonable pragmatic choice.

💬

C — Gossip-Based Decentralized Election

Gossip protocols are eventually consistent: different nodes can temporarily hold different views of who the leader is, and a partition can produce two leaders. Gossip is excellent for membership and failure detection at scale (Cassandra, and Consul's Serf layer), but it does not give the safety guarantee a single leader requires. Convergence time also grows with cluster size.

💓

D — Centralized Election with Heartbeats

A central arbiter is a single point of failure, and electing the arbiter is the original problem again. Heartbeat timeouts alone cannot tell a crashed node from a slow or partitioned one, so a falsely suspected leader may keep acting while a new one is chosen, which causes split brain unless leases and fencing are added. Heartbeats are a component of Raft, not an election strategy.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — ZooKeeper

The environment already runs ZooKeeper (Hadoop-ecosystem or older Kafka-style deployments) and election needs are modest, using ephemeral nodes and watches.

📌

B — Raft + Leader Lease

The default for replicated state machines and databases that need a single leader with strong consistency, fast failover and local reads (etcd, CockroachDB, TiKV).

📌

C — Gossip-Based

Cluster membership, failure detection and dissemination at large scale where eventual agreement is acceptable and no single leader is required.

📌

D — Centralized + Heartbeats

Non-critical, idempotent singleton jobs where a brief split brain is harmless, with fencing enforced at the resource.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Heartbeat timeouts without fencing: A leader paused by a long GC or a partition wakes up and keeps writing while a new leader exists. Fence every write with the term.
  • Leases based on wall-clock time: Clock jumps and drift break the promise. Use monotonic clocks and safety margins.
  • Even-sized or two-node clusters: They give no extra fault tolerance and can lose the majority on a single failure. Use 3 or 5 voters.
  • Badly tuned timeouts: Too low and brief network or GC hiccups cause flapping elections. Too high and failover is slow. Derive them from measured round-trip time.
  • Assuming everyone can reach the leader: Clients and followers must discover the new leader, retry and handle redirects.
  • Rolling your own consensus: Subtle bugs here lose data. Use a proven implementation.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice almost nobody writes consensus by hand. Teams delegate election to a proven consensus-backed service:

  • Step 0 — Ask whether you need a leader at all: Leaderless designs (quorum reads and writes, CRDTs) avoid election for some workloads.
  • Step 1 — Use a consensus-backed primitive: etcd election and lease APIs, Consul sessions and locks, ZooKeeper ephemeral nodes or Kubernetes Lease objects.
  • Step 2 — Embed Raft only when building a replicated store: Use a library such as etcd/raft or HashiCorp's raft, never custom code.
  • Step 3 — Fence every write: Attach the term or a fencing token and have downstream resources reject lower values, so a paused or partitioned old leader cannot do harm.
  • Step 4 — Tune timeouts: Heartbeat interval near the round-trip time and election timeout at least ten times larger (etcd's guidance), accounting for GC pauses and cross-zone latency.
  • Step 5 — Test the failure modes: Partition, crash and clock-skew chaos tests (Jepsen-style) and regular failover drills.

Correctness under partitions comes from the protocol, not from fast heartbeats.

💡

Key Takeaway

Elect leaders with a consensus protocol (Raft or an equivalent), add leases for low-latency local reads and stability, and fence with terms. Accept that availability requires a majority: during a partition you can have a safe single leader or an always-reachable one, not both.
