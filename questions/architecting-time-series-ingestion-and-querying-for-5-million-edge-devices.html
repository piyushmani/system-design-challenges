Architecting Time-Series Ingestion and Querying for 5 Million Edge Devices

Ethan is building the backend of a monitoring platform fed by 5 million edge devices, each sending 1 to 5 data points per second, which is roughly 5 to 25 million points per second at steady state. The system needs continuous high-rate ingestion, dashboards and alerts within a few seconds, complex analytics over months of history, predictable storage cost, and reliable retention and rollups. Queries fall into two clear classes: recent, low-latency (last hours to days) and historical, analytical (weeks to months). Which architecture best balances scalability, availability, query speed and operational overhead?

A
Rely on a single relational database (like Postgres) utilizing time-partitioned tables (hourly/daily), b-tree indexes on (device_id, timestamp), materialized views for frequent aggregations, and standard VACUUM or partition dropping for data retention. Scale operations vertically and through read replicas.
✗ Not Best
B
Direct ingestion into a durable append-only log (such as Kafka) -> stream recent data points into a time-series optimized datastore (like TimescaleDB or a dedicated TSDB) to handle low-latency queries and alerting; execute a streaming processor (Beam/Flink) to generate rolled-up summaries and raw cold data segments as Parquet files in object storage; employ a query router to direct recent range queries to the TSDB and analytical queries on older data to an engine (Presto/Trino) reading the Parquet files.
✓ Best Answer
C
Keep metrics in a distributed wide-column database (e.g., Cassandra) by applying a composite partition key such as (device_id, date) and clustering by the timestamp. Utilize TTLs to manage data expiration, and execute regular compaction routines alongside secondary-index aggregations to serve queries.
✗ Not Best
D
Route all incoming data through Kafka and perform periodic batch ETL processes (every hour) to dump everything into a unified columnar OLAP database (like BigQuery or ClickHouse) partitioned by day. Handle all dashboard and alert queries exclusively from the OLAP store, tolerating somewhat higher latencies for the most recent data.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — B: Log + Hot TSDB + Rollups + Parquet Cold Tier

Option B treats ingestion, hot queries and analytics as three different workloads and gives each its own engine, connected by a durable log. At 5 to 25 million points per second, no single store is good at all three, and the log makes every downstream component replaceable and replayable. Here's why it stands out:

  • A durable log absorbs the firehose: Kafka (or a similar log) decouples device bursts from the databases, provides backpressure and replay, and lets alerting, the TSDB and the rollup jobs consume the same stream independently.
  • The hot store matches the alerting SLO: a TSDB holding only recent data (hours to days) keeps indexes small, compression high and queries fast, which is how dashboards and alerts stay within seconds.
  • Cost stays predictable: raw data ages into compressed Parquet on object storage at a fraction of the cost of database disks, and rollups (1-minute, 1-hour) shrink long-range queries by orders of magnitude.
  • Analytics get the right engine: Trino or Presto over partitioned Parquet scales out for months of history and reads only the columns and partitions it needs.
  • Retention is mostly file lifecycle: dropping old hot partitions and applying object-store lifecycle rules replaces expensive row-level deletes.
  • Back-of-envelope check: 5 million devices at just 1 point per second is already about 430 billion points per day, so the design has to scale out horizontally at every tier.

Design around partitioning by device and time, idempotent writes, tiered retention (raw for days, 1-minute for weeks, 1-hour for years) and backpressure. Monitor consumer lag, hot-store p99 and cost per billion points; if lag grows, scale consumers before scaling the TSDB.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🐘

A — Single Postgres with Partitions

Time-partitioned tables and materialized views work well up to hundreds of thousands of rows per second, but 5 to 25 million per second is far beyond a single primary even with batching, because every row pays for a B-tree index on (device_id, timestamp), WAL and vacuum. Read replicas do not help writes, and vertical scaling hits a wall quickly. Materialized view refreshes over this volume are expensive, and keeping months of history in row storage is costly. Caveat: this is a fine design for thousands of devices.

🧱

C — Cassandra with (device_id, date) Keys

Cassandra sustains very high write rates and TTLs are convenient, but it is a poor fit for the query side. It has no native rollups, secondary-index aggregations scale badly, and months-long analytics across many devices means scanning many partitions or running external Spark jobs. Expired TTL data becomes tombstones that need compaction; time-window compaction mitigates this but still adds operational work. Caveat: as the hot tier behind a log it is a legitimate choice, but on its own it fails the analytics requirement.

⏱️

D — Hourly Batch ETL into OLAP

An OLAP store such as ClickHouse or BigQuery is excellent for analytics, but loading it with hourly batches means dashboards and alerts are up to an hour stale, which contradicts the requirement for results within seconds. Making the OLAP store the only serving layer also pushes alert-style lookups onto an engine tuned for scans. Caveat: streaming the log continuously into ClickHouse would repair the latency problem and is a reasonable simplification of Option B at smaller scale, but that is not what this option describes.

⚡

B — Log + Hot TSDB + Rollups + Parquet Cold Tier ✓

Direct ingestion into a durable append-only log (such as Kafka) -> stream recent data points into a time-series optimized datastore (like TimescaleDB or a dedicated TSDB) to handle low-latency queries

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Single Postgres

Thousands of devices, modest write rates, short retention and a team that already runs Postgres. Excellent for early-stage products or internal tools.

📌

B — Log + Hot TSDB + Parquet

Millions of devices or series, strict seconds-level alerting plus long-range analytics, and a team able to operate a streaming platform (or buy it managed).

📌

C — Cassandra

Very high write volume with key-based access (fetch one device's recent points) and simple queries, usually as the hot tier or a serving store rather than the whole stack.

📌

D — Hourly Batch into OLAP

Analytics-first workloads where a delay of minutes to hours is acceptable, such as reporting and capacity planning without tight alerting needs.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Unbounded cardinality in the hot store: free-form tags (request IDs, firmware strings) multiply series count and memory use. Keep tags to a controlled set.
  • Hot partitions from bad keys: keying only by timestamp or by a few large tenants overloads a few partitions. Partition by device_id (or a hash) and bucket by time.
  • Treating arrival time as event time: edge devices buffer and reconnect, so late data is routine. Use event time with watermarks and set the late-arrival window deliberately.
  • Non-idempotent writes: consumer retries and replays duplicate points. Use deterministic keys or dedup tokens.
  • Too many tiny Parquet files: frequent small writes wreck query performance. Compact into files of roughly 128 MB to 1 GB.
  • Querying raw data for long ranges: a six-month chart scanning raw points is slow and expensive. Always route long ranges to rollups.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Teams that run this at scale separate the paths and let each tier evolve on its own. A typical sequence:

  • Step 0 — Size it: estimate points per second, bytes per point, series count and the query mix. Choose retention tiers from the real access pattern.
  • Step 1 — Edge to log: devices batch and compress, then send through a stateless ingest gateway into partitioned Kafka topics keyed by device_id.
  • Step 2 — Hot store: consumers write batches to a clustered TSDB with short retention for dashboards and alerts.
  • Step 3 — Alerting: run threshold and anomaly rules on the stream or the hot store, never on the cold tier.
  • Step 4 — Rollups: a stream processor emits 1-minute and 1-hour aggregates, using watermarks for late data.
  • Step 5 — Cold tier: write raw data as Parquet partitioned by date and device bucket, and expose it through Trino or Presto.
  • Step 6 — Route queries: choose the engine and resolution by time range, so a six-month trend reads 1-hour rollups instead of raw points.

Add tiers only when measurements demand them. At smaller scale, a single clustered OLAP engine fed by Kafka may be enough.

💡

Key Takeaway

Split the problem by latency and access pattern: a log for durability, a small hot store for seconds-level queries, and cheap columnar files for history. Rollups and a query router keep costs predictable as the data grows.
