Riley's Push/Pull/Fanout Dilemma: Balancing Scale and Real-Time Delivery

Riley, lead backend architect for a social platform with 100 million users and a highly skewed follower distribution (most users have small audiences; a few have millions of followers), must design a notification system for follow, like, and post events delivered to the in-app inbox and to users' devices. Requirements: limited operational cost, high availability, low end-to-end latency for most users, near real-time delivery to online devices, deferred delivery for offline users, and fault tolerance with clear observability. Riley must choose among pull, push, and fanout strategies while handling the celebrity problem (write amplification), backpressure, and duplicates. Which strategy gives the best balance of scalability, latency, cost, and operational ease?

A
A push-first, universal fanout-on-write approach. Maintain persistent WebSocket connections for all active clients. When an event occurs, immediately distribute it by writing a separate message to a push broker for every single follower's device. Minimize per-user storage on the server, depending primarily on real-time push delivery and temporary buffering to handle retries.
✗ Not Best
B
A pull-only, fanout-on-read model. Store each event a single time in a dense event store without pre-generating individual user inboxes. Clients regularly poll an endpoint dedicated to their feed. During the read request, the service dynamically generates the relevant notifications for the user by applying filters, removing duplicates, and paginating the results, while using caching to mitigate sudden load surges.
✗ Not Best
C
A hybrid architecture. Use an asynchronous message bus and sharded workers to precompute individual inboxes (fanout-on-write) for standard users. However, for "celebrity" accounts with massive followings, switch to a fanout-on-read approach to prevent write amplification. Deliver notifications to active devices via WebSockets, APNs, or FCM, and store notifications for offline users. Incorporate idempotent writes, rate-limiting, backpressure mechanisms, and partition data per user to ensure scalability and adequate observability.
✓ Best Answer
D
A centralized push gateway with topic-based partitioning. Aggregate notifications into broad topics, utilizing a central gateway to batch and transmit push notifications to web push or device services like FCM and APNs. Eliminate per-user storage and follower-specific fanout by utilizing topic subscriptions and device tokens, scaling the gateway through consistent-hash partitioning.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: Hybrid Fanout (Write for Normal Users, Read for Celebrities)

The follower distribution is the whole problem. For the median user, precomputing inboxes is cheap and makes reads O(1). For a celebrity, the same strategy turns one post into millions of writes and delays everyone else's notifications behind the burst. A hybrid applies fanout-on-write where the fan-out is small and fanout-on-read where it is huge, and uses presence-aware delivery so you do not pay to push to devices that are offline. Note that likes and follows have a single recipient (the target user), so only post-type events actually fan out. Here's why C stands out:

  • Bounded write amplification: Accounts above a follower threshold are stored once in a celebrity feed. A post from a 50-million-follower account becomes one write instead of 50 million inbox writes.
  • Fast reads for almost everyone: Normal users read a precomputed inbox. Users who follow celebrities merge in a small number of cached celebrity feeds, so read cost is bounded by how many celebrities they follow, not by total followers.
  • Delivery matches presence: Online devices get near-real-time delivery over WebSocket (or APNs/FCM for background), while offline users get stored notifications delivered on reconnect. Celebrity push is tiered (recently active first) and throttled.
  • Resilience under bursts: A message bus with partitioned workers absorbs spikes. Backpressure (bounded queues), retries with dead-letter queues, and per-provider rate limits keep a celebrity post from degrading ordinary traffic.
  • Correctness and observability: Deterministic notification ids plus unique-key inserts make fan-out idempotent, so at-least-once delivery never shows duplicates. Fan-out lag, queue depth, push success rate, and p99 delivery latency by user tier give clear signals.

Test the design with a game-day: simulate a celebrity post to tens of millions of followers during normal traffic and verify that p99 delivery for ordinary users does not move. Track fan-out lag per partition, queue depth, push success and duplicate rates, and end-to-end latency by user tier.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

📣

A — Universal Fanout-on-Write Push

Simple and fast for small audiences, but a single post from a multi-million-follower account becomes millions of broker messages. The burst causes head-of-line blocking that delays unrelated users, saturates push gateways, and inflates cost (including pushes to inactive devices). Minimal server-side storage also weakens history, offline delivery, and de-duplication. Caveat: it is a fine design for platforms where audiences are uniformly small.

🔄

B — Pull-Only Fanout-on-Read

Storage-efficient with no write amplification, but every poll triggers filtering, de-duplication, and pagination work, so read load scales with users × poll frequency regardless of activity. Latency is bounded by the polling interval, which is not near real-time, and frequent polling drains mobile batteries. Per-user feeds have low cache hit rates. Pull is the right approach for the celebrity path, not for the whole system.

📡

D — Topic-Based Central Push Gateway

Topics work well for broadcast (breaking news, announcements), but personal social notifications are per-user. With no per-user inbox you lose unread counts, history, de-duplication, per-user preferences, and offline retrieval, and provider topic limits and delivery guarantees are weak for this use. A central gateway also concentrates failure risk. Use topics for broadcast-style messages alongside C, not instead of it.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Universal Fanout-on-Write

Platforms with uniformly small audiences (private groups, small teams) where write amplification is bounded and real-time delivery is the priority.

📌

B — Pull-Only Fanout-on-Read

Low-frequency, non-urgent feeds, or the celebrity portion of a hybrid system. Also a fallback path when push is unavailable.

📌

C — Hybrid

Skewed social graphs with a long tail of huge accounts, mixed real-time and offline delivery, and cost sensitivity.

📌

D — Topic-Based Gateway

Broadcast announcements (breaking news, system notices, sports scores) where all subscribers get the same message and no per-user history is needed.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Fixing the threshold by guesswork: Derive it from write cost versus read-merge cost on real data, and add hysteresis.
  • Non-idempotent inbox writes: Retries then create duplicates. Use deterministic notification ids and unique constraints.
  • Pushing to everyone on a celebrity post: It floods APNs/FCM and wakes inactive devices. Tier by recent activity and throttle.
  • Unbounded queues: Without backpressure a burst becomes a memory incident. Bound queues and surface lag as a metric.
  • Skipping notification collapsing: Users get 40 pushes for one popular post. Collapse and aggregate where it makes sense.
  • Ignoring ordering and cursor consistency in merged feeds: Use stable sort keys (timestamp plus id) and cursor tokens that cover both sources.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

This is the pattern behind most large timeline and notification systems, which evolve in stages rather than start hybrid. A typical path looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Measure the graph: Plot the follower distribution and event rates, and find where the long tail starts. The threshold should come from data.
  • Step 1 — Event bus and inboxes: Partitioned bus, idempotent inbox writes, and a simple fanout-on-write path for all users.
  • Step 2 — Delivery layer: WebSocket gateway with presence, plus APNs/FCM for background and offline devices.
  • Step 3 — Celebrity path: Introduce the celebrity store and read-time merge once write amplification starts to hurt.
  • Step 4 — Protect the system: Per-partition backpressure, per-provider rate limits, retry/dead-letter handling, and notification collapsing.
  • Step 5 — Tune continuously: Adjust thresholds, caching, and push tiers using lag and cost dashboards.

Optimize for the shape of your graph, not for an average user.

💡

Key Takeaway

Use a hybrid fanout: precompute inboxes for normal accounts, read-merge celebrity events at query time, and deliver through presence-aware push with idempotent writes, rate limits, and backpressure. It caps write amplification, keeps latency low for most users, and stays operable under bursts.
