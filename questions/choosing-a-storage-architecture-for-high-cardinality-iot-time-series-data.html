Choosing a Storage Architecture for High-Cardinality IoT Time-Series Data

Ethan leads the backend team at a firm collecting telemetry from roughly 200,000 edge devices, each sending 6 to 10 numeric metrics per second. That is about 1.2 to 2 million data points per second, or roughly 100 to 170 billion per day. Dashboards need sub-second to low-second queries over the most recent 5 to 15 minutes, analysts need ad-hoc and trend queries across months or years, and rare investigations need full-resolution scans over 30 to 90 days. The data has high cardinality (device, metric, tags), storage cost matters, and the platform must survive a regional outage. He needs the architecture that best balances ingest scale, fast recent queries, cheap long-term storage, availability, and observability.

A
Use a single, strongly consistent relational database (sharded PostgreSQL or MySQL) with time-based partitioning and transactional writes, keep full-resolution raw data for the entire retention period, and maintain materialized views for aggregates.
✗ Not Best
B
Use a fully managed time-series database (e.g., InfluxCloud or TimescaleDB) in one primary region with native downsampling and retention, ingest through a synchronous API, and add a read replica to serve dashboards.
✗ Not Best
C
Write all raw events to object storage (e.g., S3) as append-only blobs, compute aggregates with scheduled batch ETL, and query the data lake with Trino/Presto, accepting minutes-to-hours latency for most queries.
✗ Not Best
D
Build a tiered hot/warm/cold architecture: ingest into a write-heavy store (DynamoDB, Cassandra, or similar) with a TTL for the hot window, use a Kafka plus Flink/Beam stream layer to downsample and archive full-resolution data to S3 as Parquet, and keep pre-computed recent rollups plus a thin index for dashboards.
✓ Best Answer
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — D: Tiered Hot/Warm/Cold Architecture

Each requirement in this scenario pulls on a different storage property: write throughput (1.2 to 2 million points per second), low-latency reads on a tiny recent window, cheap capacity for years of data, and cross-region durability. No single store optimizes all four, so the right answer is a tiered hot/warm/cold architecture that puts each slice of data on the storage that suits how it is queried. Dashboards only touch the last 5 to 15 minutes and full-resolution scans are rare, so most of the data can sit in cheap columnar object storage while a small, hot, pre-aggregated window serves the interactive queries. Here's why it stands out:

  • Ingest is decoupled and buffered: Kafka absorbs bursts and partitions by device, so devices never wait on a database, consumers can fall behind and catch up, and raw events can be replayed to fix a bug or rebuild a rollup.
  • Dashboards read pre-computed rollups: A dashboard panel needs tens of points, not millions of raw samples. Stream-computed 1s/10s/1m rollups plus a thin index (device to series) deliver sub-second reads without scanning raw data.
  • Long-term storage is cheap and queryable: Parquet on S3 (ideally under an Iceberg or Delta table format) is columnar, compressed, and costs a small fraction of provisioned database storage. Even at an optimistic 16 bytes per raw point (timestamp plus value, before series keys and compression), 90 days is roughly 150 to 250 TB, which is what rules out keeping everything on database disks.
  • Resilience is built from replaceable pieces: Cross-region replication of the log (e.g., MirrorMaker 2), S3 cross-region replication, and a multi-DC hot store (Cassandra, or DynamoDB global tables) mean a regional outage degrades freshness rather than durability, and Kafka retention plus S3 can rebuild any derived tier.
  • Observability comes naturally: Consumer lag, event-time watermark delay, and the age of the latest data point per tier give direct freshness SLOs, so ingest problems show up before dashboards go blank.

A production-grade version includes idempotent writes (deterministic keys such as device + metric + timestamp), overlapping tier boundaries (hot TTL longer than rollup lag), file compaction on S3, and tier-aware query routing. Track end-to-end freshness, consumer lag, and cost per million points per tier; if recent-query latency or cost drifts, adjust the hot window and rollup resolutions before adding another store.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🗄️

A — Sharded Relational Database

A relational database offers strong transactions that telemetry does not need: the data is append-only and rarely updated. At 1.2 to 2 million inserts per second, even well-batched COPY or multi-row inserts require many shards, each maintaining indexes on a high-cardinality key that slow every write. Keeping full-resolution data for the whole retention period on database disks is the most expensive tier available, and materialized-view refreshes over billions of rows compete with ingest for the same resources. Cross-region replication of an OLTP store adds lag and failover complexity. Caveat: at a few tens of thousands of points per second, PostgreSQL with time partitioning is a simple and excellent choice.

⏱️

B — Managed Time-Series Database in One Region

A managed TSDB is a strong fit for mid-scale telemetry and offers native downsampling, but this design has two structural gaps. Everything lives in one region, and a read replica in that region does not help during a regional outage. Synchronous ingestion also couples 200,000 devices directly to database availability: a slowdown turns into timeouts and data loss at the edge unless a buffer is added. High-cardinality series and full-resolution retention are also where managed TSDB bills and index sizes grow fastest. Caveat: if volume were 10 to 50 times smaller and one region were acceptable, this would be the simplest, lowest-operations answer.

🪣

C — Object Storage With Batch ETL

This is the right design for the cold tier and the wrong design for the whole system. Object storage plus Trino is cheap, scales almost without limit, and handles ad-hoc historical scans well, but batch ETL and a lake query engine answer in minutes to hours, which misses the sub-second dashboard requirement for the last 5 to 15 minutes. Streaming many small objects also creates a small-files problem that degrades query performance unless compaction runs continuously. Caveat: for purely offline analytics with no interactive dashboards, this is the cheapest and simplest option.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Sharded Relational DB

Moderate write rates (tens of thousands of points per second), strong relational requirements such as joins with business data, and a team that already runs PostgreSQL or MySQL well.

📌

B — Managed TSDB, One Region

Mid-scale telemetry in a single region where regional outages are tolerable, the team wants minimal operations, and series cardinality stays within the vendor's comfortable range.

📌

C — Object Storage + Batch ETL

Offline analytics, compliance archives, or ML feature pipelines where results can arrive minutes or hours later and no interactive dashboards exist. Also the right choice for the cold tier of a larger design.

📌

D — Tiered Hot/Warm/Cold

Millions of points per second, high cardinality, interactive dashboards on recent data, long retention for trends, rare full-resolution scans, and a requirement to survive a regional outage.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Unbounded tag cardinality: Putting request IDs, firmware strings, or free-text values in tags multiplies series count and blows up the hot store, the index, and the rollups. Set cardinality budgets and enforce them at ingest.
  • Partitioning by time only: All current writes land on the newest partition, creating a hot spot. Partition by device bucket plus a time window.
  • Treating a wide-column store as an analytics engine: Cross-device aggregations over raw rows are slow and expensive there. Serve them from rollups or an OLAP engine.
  • Ignoring late and out-of-order data: Devices reconnect and flush buffered samples. Use event-time watermarks with allowed lateness, and keep a path to recompute rollups from raw data.
  • The small-files problem on S3: Streaming writers that flush tiny files per minute cripple Trino and Athena. Batch by size or age and compact to a few hundred MB per file.
  • Non-idempotent writes: Kafka consumers are at-least-once, so replays create duplicates unless keys are deterministic (device + metric + timestamp) or deduplicated downstream.
  • No overlap between tiers: If the hot TTL is shorter than rollup lag plus archive lag, dashboards show gaps. Keep the hot window comfortably longer than the slowest downstream tier.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, teams build this architecture from the bottom up, adding a tier only when a measured requirement demands it. A typical sequence looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Size the problem: Measure points per second, series cardinality, payload size, retention, and the real query mix. Decide which queries are interactive (seconds) and which are analytical (minutes are fine).
  • Step 1 — Buffer first: Put a durable log (Kafka, Kinesis, or Pulsar) in front of everything, partitioned by device, and have edge agents batch and compress samples (for example one message per device every few seconds) to cut per-request overhead.
  • Step 2 — Archive raw data early: Land raw events in S3 as Parquet under Iceberg or Delta. It is the cheapest durable copy, supports replay, and makes every later tier rebuildable.
  • Step 3 — Add the hot tier and rollups: Choose the hot store by query shape (key lookups favor wide-column stores; flexible slicing favors a TSDB or OLAP engine), and stream-compute 1s/10s/1m rollups for dashboards.
  • Step 4 — Query engine for history: Use Trino, Athena, or Spark with date and device-bucket partition pruning, and compact files continuously. Serve long-range trends from 1m/1h rollups and reserve raw scans for rare investigations.
  • Step 5 — Multi-region and observability: Replicate the log and object storage, set freshness and lag SLOs, enforce cardinality budgets, and track cost per tier.

Add each tier because a measurement demands it, not because the architecture diagram looks complete.

💡

Key Takeaway

No single store fits ingest rate, recent-query latency, cheap retention, and regional resilience at once. Match each time window to the storage built for how it is queried: a buffered log for ingest, a small hot tier with rollups for dashboards, and Parquet on object storage for history. The price is operational complexity, so lean on managed services and keep raw data in S3 as the source of truth for rebuilding everything else.
