Time-Series Hot vs Cold Storage Stream Processing
Challenge

Hot and Cold Storage for 50k Writes per Second of Telemetry

Maya is the backend architect for a SaaS monitoring platform. About 100k agents each send 1-5 metrics every 10 seconds, which is 10k-50k writes per second (roughly 4 billion samples per day at the peak). Raw data must be kept for 2 years (on the order of 3 trillion samples at the peak rate), while alerts and dashboards over the last 1-6 hours need sub-second queries and analysts run daily and weekly historical analyses. She must control operational complexity and cost and avoid losing data during spikes or restarts. Which time-series storage and retrieval architecture best fits?

A
Maintain a single PostgreSQL cluster as the primary data store: save each sample as a distinct row in a normalized table, leaning on vertical scaling of CPU and memory. Implement time-based partitioning and introduce read replicas to serve dashboard queries. Manage data retention through standard VACUUM operations and scheduled dropping of old partitions.
✗ Not Best
B
Deploy a wide-column database (such as Cassandra or ScyllaDB) as the main storage solution: shard the data based on (metric_id, time_bucket), utilize TTLs for data retention, and embrace eventual consistency. Handle data aggregations either dynamically at query time or via periodic background tasks.
✗ Not Best
C
Route ingestion through a durable append-only log (like Kafka) into a stream processing engine (such as Flink or Kafka Streams) to generate near-real-time downsampled streams and materialized aggregates. Store raw historical data in cost-effective object storage (e.g., Parquet files on S3) partitioned by time and metric for extensive analytics. Simultaneously, maintain recent, 'hot' data and rapid rollups within a time-series optimized OLAP database (such as ClickHouse or TimescaleDB) to power low-latency alerts and dashboards.
✓ Best Answer
D
Implement an in-memory time-series cache (like RedisTimeSeries) as the initial write destination to ensure sub-millisecond write performance and ultra-low latency reads for real-time dashboards. Asynchronously archive this data in batches to a disk-based database during off-peak hours for long-term retention and historical querying.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: Durable Log + Rollups + Hot OLAP + Cold Parquet

The workload has three very different access patterns: continuous durable ingestion, low-latency queries on recent data, and cheap bulk scans over years of history. Option C gives each pattern the storage that suits it: a durable log for ingestion and replay, a time-series OLAP store (ClickHouse or TimescaleDB) with pre-aggregated rollups for the hot window, and columnar Parquet on object storage as the cold, cheap, long-term tier. Here's why it stands out:

  • Durable, replayable ingestion: Kafka buffers bursts, decouples agents from storage, and lets you replay to rebuild rollups or backfill a new store without asking 100k agents to resend data.
  • Fast hot queries: Columnar time-series stores with delta and Gorilla-style compression, time-ordered keys, and incremental rollups return last-hours dashboard queries in well under a second. Facebook's Gorilla paper (Pelkonen et al., VLDB 2015) reports roughly 1.4 bytes per data point on average.
  • Cheap long-term retention: Two years of raw data compresses to roughly single-digit to low double-digit terabytes in columnar form, depending on encoding, compared with hundreds of terabytes in a row store. On object storage (Parquet, partitioned by date and metric) it costs a small fraction of SSD and is queryable with Athena, Trino, or Spark.
  • Right-sized compute per query type: Alerts hit tiny rollup tables, analysts scan Parquet on demand, and ingestion scales with Kafka partitions and consumers.
  • Cost and operations stay controlled: Retention is enforced by TTL and tiering policies, rollups shrink dashboard queries by one to two orders of magnitude, and each component can fail or scale independently.

A practical build has Kafka keyed by agent or metric, idempotent writes (dedupe on agent, metric, timestamp), 1-minute and 1-hour rollups, TTL-based tiering to object storage, and a query router that picks raw data, rollups, or Parquet by time range. Monitor ingestion lag, query p99, compaction backlog, and cost per billion samples.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🐘

A — Single PostgreSQL Cluster

At the peak rate, one row per sample is about 4 billion rows per day and roughly 3 trillion over two years. Even with partitioning, row-store overhead (tuple headers, indexes) puts that in the hundreds of terabytes, index maintenance and WAL throttle ingestion, and vertical scaling hits a ceiling. Read replicas help dashboards but not storage or write cost. Caveat: PostgreSQL with TimescaleDB (hypertables, compression, continuous aggregates) is a legitimate hot-tier choice, though that is a different design from a plain normalized table.

🧬

B — Cassandra / ScyllaDB as the Main Store

Wide-column stores handle the write rate and TTL retention very well, but they are weak at analytics: aggregating across many series or scanning weeks of data needs query-time computation over partitions or an external engine such as Spark, and sub-second dashboard aggregations require carefully maintained rollup tables. TTL deletes create tombstones and compaction load, and you end up building most of option C's rollup layer yourself. Caveat: a good fit when the dominant pattern is key-based reads of raw series rather than aggregates.

⚡

D — Redis Time Series First

In-memory writes are very fast, but using Redis as the first and only landing zone makes memory the cost driver and durability a risk: data not yet persisted or archived is lost on node failure, and off-peak batch archiving adds a delayed, failure-prone second hop. There is no replayable log to recover from. Redis is a fine serving cache for the most recent minutes behind a durable log, but not a system of record.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Single PostgreSQL

Small to mid-scale telemetry (millions of rows per day) where SQL familiarity matters; consider TimescaleDB for something in between.

📌

B — Cassandra / ScyllaDB

Massive write-heavy workloads with key-based access (device state, event lookups) where multi-region availability matters more than analytics.

📌

C — Log + Rollups + Hot OLAP + Cold Parquet

High-volume telemetry with mixed needs: real-time dashboards and alerts, long retention, and occasional deep analytics, with a team able to operate a streaming pipeline.

📌

D — Redis Time Series First

Very recent, small-window serving (the last few minutes) as a cache in front of a durable store, or short-lived metrics with no long retention.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Row-at-a-time inserts: OLAP stores want batches of thousands of rows. Buffer in Kafka or the gateway and insert in bulk.
  • Unbounded tag cardinality: Putting request IDs or user IDs in tags explodes series counts, memory, and index size. Limit tag keys and values.
  • Querying raw data for long ranges: A 30-day dashboard scanning raw samples is slow and expensive. Route to rollups by time range.
  • Tiny Parquet files: Writing many small files to object storage cripples query planning. Compact into large files (hundreds of MB) partitioned by date.
  • Ignoring late and duplicate data: Agents retry and clocks drift. Dedupe on (agent, metric, timestamp) and define a late-arrival window for rollups.
  • Rollups that cannot be recomputed: Without the raw log or raw tier, a bug in aggregation logic is permanent. Keep the log or raw data long enough to rebuild.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Monitoring platforms (Prometheus-style remote storage, commercial observability systems) converge on tiering by time:

  • Step 0 — Define SLOs and queries: Which windows need sub-second reads, which resolutions are needed at which age (raw, 1 minute, 1 hour), and what ingestion lag is acceptable?
  • Step 1 — Durable ingestion: Agents write to a stateless gateway that batches into Kafka, partitioned by agent or metric, with schema and tag limits enforced.
  • Step 2 — Hot store: ClickHouse or TimescaleDB with time-ordered keys and compression, using batch inserts (thousands of rows) rather than row-at-a-time writes.
  • Step 3 — Rollups: Incremental 1-minute and 1-hour aggregates via materialized views, continuous aggregates, or a stream processor for stateful windows.
  • Step 4 — Cold tier: Move or export aged raw data to Parquet on object storage, partitioned by date and metric, and compact small files.
  • Step 5 — Query routing and cost control: Route by time range and resolution, set retention per tier, track cost per billion samples, and cap query scan sizes.

Store each age of data in the cheapest tier that still meets its query SLO.

💡

Key Takeaway

Match storage to access pattern and age. A durable log protects ingestion, a hot time-series store with rollups serves real-time dashboards, and compressed Parquet on object storage keeps two years of raw data affordable. A single relational cluster, a wide-column store used as an analytics engine, or an in-memory-first design each fail on cost, query shape, or durability.
