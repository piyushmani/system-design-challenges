Maya's Wallet Service: Balance Accuracy vs. Millisecond Latency

Maya is the backend architect for a global wallet service handling microtransactions (payments, refunds, transfers). The business requires a 99.99% uptime SLA and an SLO of 99.95% of API requests within 100 ms. The most important SLI is balance correctness: no double-spending and no lost updates. Users around the world read and write from many regions. She must pick an architecture that balances availability, latency, scalability, and correctness.

A
Operate a single, globally-distributed, strongly-consistent SQL system that executes synchronous cross-region commits for every write operation (global transactions). This approach provides a singular source of truth and straightforward correctness, although every write incurs the latency associated with cross-region commits.
✗ Not Best
B
Direct all write operations to a primary region and deploy read replicas in alternate regions utilizing asynchronous replication. Ensure read-after-write consistency by implementing sticky sessions or client-side routing to the primary; apply compensating transactions to manage conflicts. This strategy yields fast local reads but may expose clients to stale data and introduces complexities in handling failure scenarios.
✗ Not Best
C
Partition accounts by key and establish leader-based consensus (e.g., Raft) for each shard within every region: utilize synchronous replication among local replicas for the leader of each shard to guarantee strong per-account consistency and low write latency, supplemented by asynchronous cross-region replication for disaster recovery. Position leaders geographically close to the majority of a shard's active users, and incorporate health checks alongside rapid leader failover mechanisms.
✓ Best Answer
D
Position a low-latency cache (such as Redis) ahead of reads and writes, pushing write intents to a durable event log for asynchronous processing. Provide immediate success responses to clients whenever feasible and reconcile any conflicts subsequently. This architecture prioritizes low latency and high availability, but it necessitates accepting eventual consistency for account balances.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: Per-Shard Raft Groups with Regionally Placed Leaders

A wallet is a case where correctness is per account: the invariant (balance never goes negative, each operation applied exactly once) concerns one account at a time. That means you do not need global agreement on every write, only linearizable agreement within each account's shard. Partitioning accounts into Raft groups whose replicas sit in nearby zones gives strong consistency with a quorum round trip of a few milliseconds, and placing each leader near its users keeps the 100 ms SLO achievable. Cross-region asynchronous replication adds disaster recovery without taxing the write path. Here's why it stands out:

  • Strong correctness where it matters: All operations on one account go through its shard leader and commit via Raft quorum, giving a single linearizable history. A conditional update (balance >= amount) plus an idempotency key prevents double-spend and duplicate application.
  • Latency within budget: A write commits after acknowledgement from a majority of replicas in the same region (typically 1 to 3 ms across zones), so p99.95 under 100 ms is realistic, unlike any design that waits on an intercontinental round trip.
  • Scalable by construction: Accounts are independent, so throughput scales by adding shards. Hot accounts are limited by one leader, but a wallet's per-account rate is naturally modest.
  • Availability through local redundancy: Losing a node or zone triggers a Raft leader election in seconds, and replicas in multiple zones keep the shard serving. Health checks and fast failover support a 99.99% target.
  • Disaster recovery without write-path penalty: Async replication to a second region protects against region loss, with a defined RPO/RTO, while normal writes never wait on it.

Combine an immutable double-entry ledger, balance updates guarded by conditional checks, idempotency keys on every mutation, geo-aware shard placement, a rehearsed failover and DR runbook, and continuous invariant checks (ledger sum equals balance, total money conserved) as a direct measurement of the correctness SLI.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🌍

A — Global Synchronous Cross-Region Commits

It gives the simplest mental model and strong consistency, but every write pays the cost of a cross-region quorum. Inter-continental round trips commonly range from tens to a few hundred milliseconds, which makes a 100 ms p99.95 target unrealistic for global user bases and leaves little room for retries. Partitions between regions can also stall commits. Caveat: viable when the footprint is regional (for example regions within one continent) and the latency budget allows it.

🏠

B — Single Primary Region + Async Read Replicas

All writes go to one region, so users elsewhere pay long round trips (violating the latency SLO), and the primary region is a concentrated availability risk. Replicas are stale, so sticky routing or compensations are needed, and when a primary fails over to an async replica, recently acknowledged writes can be lost, which in a wallet means lost updates. Compensating transactions are a poor substitute for preventing double-spend. Caveat: a reasonable, simpler design when users are concentrated near one region and the SLA is looser.

🧪

D — Cache + Event Log with Optimistic Success

Acknowledging before validating against the authoritative balance makes double-spending possible: two concurrent debits can both succeed, and reconciliation later produces negative balances or clawbacks. That directly violates the system's most important SLI. Eventual consistency is acceptable for read-side projections, not for the decision to approve a spend. Caveat: suitable for low-value or non-monetary counters (for example loyalty points with tolerant policies), or as a read cache in front of the authoritative write path.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Global Synchronous Commits

Users within one continent or tightly grouped regions, a relaxed latency budget, and a preference for the simplest strongly consistent model.

📌

B — Single Primary + Async Replicas

Users concentrated near one region, moderate availability targets, and read-heavy traffic where stale reads are tolerable.

📌

C — Per-Shard Raft + Regional Leaders

Global user base, strict per-account correctness, tight latency SLOs, and a team able to operate a sharded consensus system or a managed equivalent.

📌

D — Cache + Event Log, Optimistic

Non-monetary or low-risk counters and read-side projections where temporary inconsistency is acceptable and corrections are cheap.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Read-modify-write without a guard: Reading a balance then writing a new one races under concurrency. Use conditional updates or serializable transactions on the shard.
  • No idempotency keys: Retries after timeouts apply a payment twice. Make every mutation idempotent and return the stored result on repeat.
  • Mutable balances with no ledger: Without append-only entries you cannot audit or reconcile. Keep an immutable ledger as the system of record.
  • Naive cross-shard transfers: Debiting one shard and crediting another without durable state loses or duplicates money on failure. Use holds, persisted saga state, and compensation, or 2PC.
  • Reading balances from followers for decisions: A stale balance can approve an overspend. Use leader or lease reads for authorization.
  • Never testing region failover: RPO and RTO are theoretical until drilled. Rehearse leader failover, zone loss, and region loss regularly.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Financial platforms separate what must be linearizable (the decision to apply a movement of money on an account) from what can be eventual (reporting, notifications, analytics). Systems like Spanner, CockroachDB, and TigerBeetle reflect variations of this principle. A typical build looks like this:

  • Step 0 — State the invariants: Balance never negative (unless overdraft is allowed), every operation applied exactly once, and total money conserved across transfers.
  • Step 1 — Model money as an append-only ledger: Double-entry records with an account balance derived or cached transactionally, and a unique idempotency key per operation.
  • Step 2 — Shard by account, one Raft group per shard: Replicas across zones in the account's home region, leader placement near users, and rebalancing when usage shifts.
  • Step 3 — Make every mutation idempotent: Clients retry on timeouts, so the same request must return the same result and never apply twice.
  • Step 4 — Handle transfers explicitly: Same-shard transfers are one transaction. Cross-shard transfers use 2PC or a saga with holds, persisted state, and compensation.
  • Step 5 — Plan DR and verify correctness continuously: Async or quorum replication to a second region with defined RPO/RTO, regular failover drills, and background checkers that compare ledger sums with balances.

Spend consistency budget only where an invariant requires it, and keep everything else eventually consistent.

💡

Key Takeaway

For money, make the correctness boundary as small as the invariant allows: one account, one consensus group, one nearby leader. That delivers strong consistency at millisecond latency, scales by sharding, and survives local failures. Treat cross-shard transfers and region-level disaster recovery as explicit design problems with idempotent steps, persisted state, and a clearly stated RPO.
