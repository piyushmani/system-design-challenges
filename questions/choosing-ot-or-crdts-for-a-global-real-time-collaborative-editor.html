Choosing OT or CRDTs for a Global Real-Time Collaborative Editor

Liam, a principal software engineer, is building a multi-tenant, real-time collaborative document editor for a major SaaS platform. The system needs low-latency edits worldwide, offline editing with fast reconnection, high availability (collaboration continues when some servers fail), per-document sharding, and operational observability (metrics, audit trails, storage compaction). He must decide on a conflict-resolution and architecture strategy: OT versus CRDTs, centralized versus decentralized. Which option best balances latency, availability, consistency, observability and long-term storage cost?

A
Use a centralized Operational Transformation (OT) service: direct all modifications through regional OT servers that apply a global transformation pipeline to ensure strict sequential consistency per document. Clients transmit small operations synchronously, receiving transformed operations in return, with documents sharded across server pools and operation history saved for audits and replays.
✗ Not Best
B
Implement CRDTs backed by a server-side durable append log and delta synchronization: clients execute CRDT logic locally and while offline to merge changes. The servers supply reliable causal broadcasting, per-document append-only logs for durability and distribution, routine garbage collection of CRDT metadata, and necessary audit hooks, using delta-CRDTs to minimize bandwidth.
✓ Best Answer
C
Adopt a purely peer-to-peer CRDT gossip architecture: clients share CRDT deltas over a WebRTC mesh without any central ordering mechanism, gossiping until state converges. Rely on local client storage for history and opportunistic peer connections to eventually save edits to the service when possible.
✗ Not Best
D
Utilize a per-document lease/leader strategy: employ a distributed coordination service to elect a temporary leader for each document, which then serializes edits for that shard. Clients forward edits to the active leader; if it fails, a new lease is established. Save the leader-serialized operations and rely on leader handoff mechanisms for failover.
✗ Not Best

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — B: CRDTs with a Durable Server Log

The hardest requirements here are offline editing, global latency and availability without a single ordering point. CRDTs are designed for exactly that: every edit is applied locally with no round trip, and merges are commutative, associative and idempotent, so any order of delivery converges. What pure CRDTs lack (durability, audit, compaction, access control, sharding) is supplied by the server-side per-document append log, which is used for distribution and storage rather than for correctness. Delta synchronization keeps bandwidth proportional to what changed. Here's why it stands out:

  • Edit latency independent of distance: Edits are applied and shown locally; the network only propagates them. With OT, clients also apply optimistically, but correctness depends on the server's ordering and acknowledgement, so everything is tied to a round trip to the document's ordering point.
  • Offline and reconnection by construction: A reconnecting client sends what it already has (a state vector) and receives only the missing deltas, then uploads its own. In OT, a long offline session must be transformed against every concurrent operation the server accepted meanwhile, which is slow and error-prone.
  • High availability without a leader: Any regional sync server (and any client) can accept edits. Losing a server only delays propagation, and clients reconnect to another region and continue. Per-document sharding of the log keeps blast radius small.
  • The log provides what observability needs: An append-only log per document gives an audit trail, replay, sync-lag metrics, and a base for snapshots and compaction. This closes the main gap of pure CRDT systems without giving up local-first editing.
  • Production precedent cuts both ways: Google Docs-style editors have long run centralized OT, Figma describes a server-authoritative design inspired by CRDTs, and Yjs and Automerge power local-first sync. The combination of offline, global and highly available requirements is what tips this scenario toward CRDTs.

A well-designed system includes per-document sharding, state-vector delta sync, snapshots plus a log tail, causal-stability garbage collection, authorization and validation at the log boundary, and a separate ephemeral channel for cursors and presence. Track sync lag, reconnect time, document size versus visible text, and compaction ratio. If the metadata-to-content ratio keeps climbing, tune compaction or revisit the data model.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

📝

A — Centralized OT Service

OT gives a single, strictly ordered history per document and compact operations, and it is proven at scale. But correctness depends on a central transformation pipeline: edits need server acknowledgement, a regional outage stalls the documents it owns, and multi-region active-active needs sticky document-to-region ownership. Long offline sessions are expensive to rebase, and transformation functions are notoriously hard to get right. Caveat: if users are almost always online and documents are mostly text, OT is simpler and has lower metadata overhead, so it is a good choice there.

🕸️

C — Pure Peer-to-Peer CRDT Gossip

There is no authoritative store, so durability depends on a peer being online, and there is no central point for tenant-level authorization, audit trails or compaction. A WebRTC mesh needs NAT traversal and relay servers, and its connection count grows quadratically with participants. It suits small private sessions, not a multi-tenant SaaS with compliance needs.

👑

D — Per-Document Leader/Lease

A leader gives a clean serialization point, but every edit pays a round trip to that leader (poor for global users), failover creates a pause while a new lease is established, and popular documents create hot leaders. Offline edits still need a merge strategy (OT or CRDT) when they rejoin, so this option does not remove the hard problem, it only relocates it. Leases also need fencing to prevent two leaders acting at once.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Centralized OT

Mostly online users, text-centric documents, strict single ordered history required, and a team using a mature OT implementation.

📌

B — CRDT + Server Log

Offline or local-first editing, multi-region active-active deployment, rich or structured content, and tolerance for extra metadata in exchange for availability.

📌

C — P2P CRDT Gossip

Small, private, ad-hoc sessions with no compliance or audit requirements and a desire to minimize server cost.

📌

D — Leader/Lease per Document

Low-edit-rate structured resources where strict serialization matters more than latency and users are close to the leader.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • No garbage collection plan: Tombstones and per-element metadata grow without bound. Define snapshot and compaction cadence on day one and measure document size against visible content size.
  • Assuming convergence means correctness: Replicas will agree, but the merged result may not match user intent (interleaved text, conflicting list moves). Use types designed for your content, such as rich-text CRDTs and move-aware lists or trees.
  • Trusting client operations: Without validation and authorization at the server, a malicious or buggy client can append oversized or unauthorized deltas, and users whose access was revoked can keep editing offline and sync later.
  • Compacting without causal stability: Dropping metadata that a lagging client still references corrupts its merge. Track a version-vector horizon and force a full resync for clients behind it.
  • Sharding by user instead of document: All editors of a document must converge through the same log shard. Hot documents with many editors need a fan-out tier and throttled awareness traffic.
  • Mixing presence into the durable log: Cursors, selections and typing indicators are ephemeral. Keep them on a separate lossy channel so they do not bloat storage or audit history.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Teams building collaborative editors quantify first and then standardize on proven components rather than inventing algorithms:

  • Step 0 — Quantify the workload: Concurrent editors per document, edit rate, document size distribution, offline duration, audit and retention rules, and whether content is plain text, rich text or structured data.
  • Step 1 — Adopt a proven CRDT library: Use Yjs, Automerge or similar rather than writing a CRDT from scratch, and pick one whose data types match your content model.
  • Step 2 — Server sync plus append log: Shard by document ID, store deltas durably before acknowledging, and take periodic snapshots so new clients load a snapshot plus a short tail.
  • Step 3 — Enforce policy at the log boundary: Authenticate and authorize each connection, validate delta size and shape, rate-limit, and isolate tenants (including per-tenant encryption keys where required).
  • Step 4 — Compaction and tiered storage: Garbage-collect at causally stable points, archive old log segments to object storage for audit, and define a policy for clients that fall behind the snapshot horizon.
  • Step 5 — Test the failure modes: Property-based convergence tests, offline fuzzing, region-failover drills, and metrics on lag, document growth and compaction ratio.

Choose the consistency model your offline and availability requirements force, then add centralized services around it for everything the model does not give you.

💡

Key Takeaway

When users must edit offline, from anywhere in the world, and keep working through server failures, local-first CRDTs backed by a durable per-document server log fit best: edits never wait on the network, merges converge, and the log supplies audit, sharding and compaction. Pay attention to metadata growth, server-side validation and garbage collection. If your users are always online and strict global ordering matters more than offline support, centralized OT remains a strong, simpler alternative.
