Challenge

Maya's WAL trade-off: write speed vs safety in a distributed DB

Maya is building a distributed, partitioned key-value database for low-latency microservices. Each partition has 3 replicas across two datacenters. The system must survive single-node crashes and the loss of a datacenter, keep p95 write latency under 10 ms, and guarantee that no acknowledged write is ever lost. SREs also need strong observability into replication lag and simple recovery procedures for failed replicas. She must choose a Write-Ahead Logging design that balances latency, availability, consistency, and operational simplicity.

A
Primary fsync-first: The leader node appends data to a local on-disk WAL and executes an fsync before sending an acknowledgment to the client. Replication to follower nodes is handled asynchronously following the acknowledgment. Latency is optimized through small write batches at the leader.
✗ Not Best
B
Centralized WAL service: All leader nodes synchronously write client updates to a single, highly available WAL cluster (operating as an independent service) that manages replication and durability. Leaders subsequently apply updates locally from this centralized log and acknowledge clients only after receiving confirmation from the central cluster.
✗ Not Best
C
Per-partition consensus-replicated WAL: Each partition employs a leader-based consensus algorithm (such as Raft) that writes to a local WAL and replicates the entries across a majority of the replicas. The leader utilizes group commit and batching techniques, acknowledging the client only once a quorum has successfully persisted the data.
✓ Best Answer
D
External distributed log as WAL: Leader nodes asynchronously write to an external distributed log (like Kafka). They acknowledge the client once the external log confirms the data has been replicated to its in-sync replicas. Local persistence and snapshotting are leveraged for rapid reads and system recovery.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: Per-Partition Consensus-Replicated WAL (Raft)

'No acknowledged write is lost' means an acknowledgment may only be sent after the entry is durable on a quorum of independent replicas, and 'survive failures' means leader failover must be automatic and safe (no split-brain, no loss of committed entries). A consensus-replicated log provides both properties by construction, and keeping it per partition limits the blast radius. Here's why it stands out:

  • Durability by quorum: An entry commits only after a majority has persisted (fsynced) it. Raft's election rule guarantees any new leader already holds every committed entry, so acknowledged writes survive node crashes and failovers.
  • Low latency through batching: Group commit amortizes one fsync across many writes. The leader writes its local WAL in parallel with sending AppendEntries, so commit time is roughly the slowest member of the fastest quorum (local fsync, or follower round trip plus its fsync), typically a few milliseconds on NVMe with power-loss protection.
  • Automatic, safe failover: Terms and elections fence old leaders. No external coordinator or human promotion step is required, which shortens recovery time and removes a class of split-brain bugs.
  • Observability and simple recovery: The log index gives crisp signals: commit index, applied index, per-follower match-index lag, fsync latency, leader changes. Recovery is snapshot plus log replay, and a replacement replica joins as a non-voting learner, catches up, then is promoted.
  • Failure isolation: Each partition has its own log and leader, so one hot or unhealthy partition does not take down the rest, unlike a shared central log.

Define commit precisely as durable on a majority, including at least one replica outside the leader's datacenter. Track fsync p99, commit-to-apply lag, and follower match-index lag, and rehearse leader-kill, datacenter-loss, and replica-rebuild drills regularly.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

💾

A — Leader fsync-First, Async Replication

Fast and simple, but acknowledging before any follower has the data means a leader crash or datacenter loss can lose acknowledged writes (RPO greater than zero). That directly violates the requirement. Local fsync protects only against process crashes, not machine or site loss, and asynchronous failover needs promotion logic that risks divergent histories.

🏛️

B — Centralized WAL Service

It can be durable, but it makes every partition's write path depend on one shared service: an extra network hop on each write, a shared blast radius (an overloaded log cluster degrades all partitions), and a log service that itself needs consensus, so the problem has moved rather than gone away. Leaders still need fencing. Shared-log designs can work at very large scale with a dedicated platform team, but they add operational complexity beyond what these requirements justify.

📨

D — External Distributed Log (Kafka-Style) as WAL

Durable when configured with acks=all and a minimum in-sync replica count of 2 or more, but it adds a second replicated system to every write: another network hop and batching delay that threatens p95 under 10 ms, two leader-election domains (database leader and log-partition leader) that must be fenced against each other, and offset-versus-state reconciliation during recovery. Kafka also relies on replication rather than per-write fsync for durability by default, and you now operate and observe two stacks.

⚡

C — Per-Partition Consensus-Replicated WAL (Raft) ✓

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Leader fsync, Async Replication

Caches, non-critical stores, or systems where losing the last moments of data on failover is acceptable, and as the base for an asynchronous disaster-recovery copy.

📌

B — Centralized WAL Service

A platform team building a shared-log foundation for many services, with the staffing to run it, isolate tenants, and engineer it for very high availability.

📌

C — Per-Partition Raft WAL

The default for strongly consistent stores that need zero acknowledged-write loss and automatic failover, such as metadata services and transactional key-value databases.

📌

D — External Log (Kafka-Style)

Event-sourced systems where the log is the system of record and state is rebuilt from it, and where the latency budget is looser than 10 ms.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Acknowledging before fsync on a majority: Writing to the page cache, or using disks with volatile write caches and no power-loss protection, loses 'committed' data on power failure.
  • Ignoring quorum placement: A majority living in one datacenter means that site's loss can destroy acknowledged writes or halt the partition.
  • One fsync per write: Without group commit, latency and throughput collapse under load.
  • Unbounded log growth and slow snapshots: Recovery takes ages, and follower catch-up can saturate the network. Compact regularly and throttle rebuilds.
  • Serving stale reads as linearizable: Reads from a follower or a deposed leader need ReadIndex or a valid leader lease.
  • Rolling your own consensus: Subtle bugs (commit rules, membership changes, snapshot races) are very hard to find without a proven implementation and Jepsen-style testing.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, teams start from the failure model and then choose replication topology, rather than the other way around. A typical sequence looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Define the failure model: Which domains can fail (node, rack, datacenter), required RPO (zero here), and target RTO.
  • Step 1 — Choose placement: Three failure domains or a 5-replica layout (2+2+1) so that losing any one site still leaves a majority, with a datacenter-aware commit rule.
  • Step 2 — Use a proven consensus library: etcd/raft, Dragonboat, or Apache Ratis, rather than writing consensus from scratch.
  • Step 3 — Engineer the log device: Dedicated NVMe with power-loss protection, fdatasync or O_DSYNC, preallocated segments, checksums, and torn-write detection.
  • Step 4 — Snapshots and rebuilds: Periodic snapshots with log compaction, learner-based replica replacement, and throttled catch-up so recovery does not starve foreground traffic.
  • Step 5 — Observe and rehearse: Dashboards for fsync latency and replication lag, plus chaos drills: kill the leader, partition a datacenter, slow a disk.

Durability is a property of where the quorum lives, not just of fsync.

💡

Key Takeaway

To guarantee that no acknowledged write is lost while failing over automatically, acknowledge only after a quorum of independent replicas has durably logged the entry. A per-partition Raft log with group commit does this at low latency, but only if replicas are placed across enough failure domains that a datacenter loss still leaves a durable majority.
