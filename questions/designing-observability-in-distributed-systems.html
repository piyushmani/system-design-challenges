Designing Observability in Distributed Systems

Emily, a senior software engineer, is architecting a new microservices platform and must choose the optimal observability approach among logging, tracing and monitoring. Every service produces a massive volume of requests and responses, so telemetry itself becomes a scaling and cost problem: full-fidelity logs and traces would add significant CPU, network and storage overhead and risk saturating the ingestion pipeline. Basic service metrics and trace-context propagation are already in place. The open question is how to keep the data needed for debugging without drowning in volume, so she must decide what to evaluate and prioritize.

A
Set up centralized logging to collect and combine logs from every service to simplify analysis.
✗ Not Best
B
Adopt distributed tracing to follow requests across multiple services and gain granular insights.
✗ Not Best
C
Establish monitoring that tracks metrics such as memory and CPU usage to guarantee performance and health.
✗ Not Best
D
Utilize log sampling to minimize overhead while retaining crucial data necessary for debugging.
✓ Best Answer
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — D: Sampling to Control Telemetry Volume

At this scale the binding constraint is not which signal exists but how much telemetry you can afford to emit, ship, store and query. Keeping everything is rarely feasible, and the useful fraction is small: errors, slow requests and a representative baseline. Sampling with rules that always keep the important events cuts overhead by orders of magnitude while preserving what debugging needs, and it lets logs, traces and metrics each do the job they are cheapest at. Here's why it stands out:

  • Overhead and cost under control: Telemetry cost scales with volume. Keeping a few percent of healthy traffic cuts storage, ingestion and query cost roughly in proportion, and reduces CPU, network and pipeline load on the services themselves.
  • Smart sampling keeps what matters: Always keep errors, slow requests and flagged sessions, and sample fast successful requests at a low rate. Tail-based sampling decides after the trace completes, so rare failures are not lost (head-based sampling is cheaper but blind to the outcome).
  • Metrics stay unsampled: Aggregated counters and histograms (RED: rate, errors, duration; USE for resources) cost almost nothing regardless of traffic and give exact rates and alerts. Sampling only affects the high-cardinality detail, so dashboards and SLOs stay accurate.
  • Correlation survives: Propagate a trace id (W3C Trace Context), put it in every log line and sample consistently by trace id, so a kept trace has its logs and vice versa.
  • Why not just tracing (B)? Tracing answers where a request spent its time, but tracing every request at this volume is itself expensive. Sampling is the decision that makes tracing affordable.
  • Proven at scale: Google's Dapper paper (2010) describes sampling as essential to keep tracing overhead negligible, and OpenTelemetry's Collector offers tail-sampling processors for exactly this purpose.

A balanced design uses metrics for always-on, exact signals, traces sampled with tail-based rules for request flow, and structured, level-aware logs carrying trace ids, with sampling rates visible in the data and audit logs excluded from sampling. Review cost per signal and adjust rates as traffic changes.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

📚

A — Centralized Logging

Centralizing logs is necessary, but collecting everything from every service at massive request volume is expensive (ingest, indexing, storage, slow queries) and noisy. Logs also lack cross-service causality unless every line carries a trace id. It is a transport and storage choice that still needs a volume-control policy such as sampling, log levels and retention tiers.

🧵

B — Distributed Tracing

Traces show how a single request flows across services with per-hop latency, which is the best tool for finding where time is spent. But tracing every request has real overhead (span creation, propagation, export) and storage cost at massive volume, so in practice tracing is enabled together with sampling. It answers where a request went, not whether the system is healthy. Caveat: once sampling exists, tracing is the highest-value signal to add for debugging microservices.

📊

C — Metrics Monitoring (CPU, Memory)

Metrics are cheap, aggregate well and drive alerting, and every system needs them. But host-level metrics such as CPU and memory show that something is wrong, not which request, endpoint or dependency is at fault. Request-level metrics (rate, errors, duration) are far more useful than resource metrics, and neither gives per-request debugging context.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Centralized Logging

Always needed as the storage and search backbone, combined with structured logs, retention tiers and level-based sampling. Required for audit and security logs.

📌

B — Distributed Tracing

Microservices with many hops where latency and failures must be attributed to specific services. Pair it with sampling.

📌

C — Metrics Monitoring

The foundation of every system: health, capacity and SLO alerting. Start here.

📌

D — Sampling

High-throughput services where full-fidelity telemetry is too costly: keep all errors and slow requests, sample the rest.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Uniform random sampling that drops rare errors: The failure you need to debug is exactly the one most likely to be discarded. Always keep errors and slow requests.
  • Inconsistent sampling across signals and services: If each service decides independently, traces arrive with missing spans and logs do not match traces. Sample by trace id and honor the parent's decision.
  • Computing rates from sampled data: Counts and percentages become wrong. Use unsampled metrics, or record the sampling rate and re-weight.
  • Head-based sampling only: The decision is made before the outcome is known, so it cannot prefer failures. Add tail-based rules where it matters.
  • Sampling audit or security logs: Those need complete retention on a separate pipeline.
  • High-cardinality metric labels: Putting user or request ids in labels explodes metric storage. Keep that detail in traces and logs.
  • Unstructured logs without trace ids: They cannot be correlated with traces, so sampling decisions cannot be applied consistently.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

The three signals are complementary, and mature teams budget telemetry the way they budget any other resource:

  • Step 0 — Define the questions: Set SLOs, list the incidents you need to be able to debug, and decide a budget and retention per signal.
  • Step 1 — Metrics first: RED metrics per service and endpoint, USE metrics for resources, SLO-based alerts and low-cardinality labels.
  • Step 2 — Standardize instrumentation: OpenTelemetry SDKs, W3C Trace Context propagation and structured JSON logs that include the trace id.
  • Step 3 — Sample deliberately: Head sampling at the SDK for the baseline, tail sampling in a collector tier for errors and slow traces, dynamic rate limits per service and level-based log sampling.
  • Step 4 — Tier storage: Hot searchable storage for days, cheap object storage for weeks or months, and a separate pipeline for audit logs.
  • Step 5 — Watch the pipeline itself: Dropped spans, queue depth and cost per service, with a periodic review of sampling rates.

Observability is a product with a budget: decide what you need to know, then pay only for that.

💡

Key Takeaway

At massive scale, telemetry volume is the design constraint. Keep metrics exact and always on, correlate everything with a trace id, and use smart sampling (keep all errors and slow requests, sample the rest) so debugging data survives without crushing cost or performance.
