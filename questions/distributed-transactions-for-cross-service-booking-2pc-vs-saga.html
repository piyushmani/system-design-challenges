Distributed Transactions for Cross-Service Booking: 2PC vs SAGA

Marco, a senior software engineer, is building an online travel booking platform where a single booking spans the Inventory, Payment, Reservation and Notification services. The platform handles thousands of requests per second, must stay highly available across regions, and must give users a clear outcome: either a confirmed booking or a definite failure with a refund. He needs a distributed-transaction approach that prevents partial bookings while meeting the availability and latency requirements. Which architecture is the most appropriate?

A
Employ a distributed 2PC (XA) transaction coordinator to unite all services under a single global transaction. Maintain rigorous ACID properties across services and lock resources until the commit or rollback is finished.
✗ Not Best
B
Develop an orchestrated SAGA pattern: utilize a central coordinator to trigger each phase (inventory reservation, payment processing, state persistence, notification dispatch). Services should use the outbox pattern for writing state, ensure idempotent steps, and define compensating actions for failures, while tracking the saga's state in a persistent store for monitoring and retries.
✓ Best Answer
C
Adopt a choreographed (event-driven) SAGA: broadcast domain events from each service (reserve -> payment -> persist) relying on eventual consistency. Eliminate the central coordinator, allowing services to respond to events and execute compensations by generating compensating events when necessary.
✗ Not Best
D
Forgo distributed transactions entirely: consolidate cross-service data into a centralized relational database (or utilize distributed locking on a shared DB) to execute the booking process within a single local ACID transaction, thereby bypassing cross-service coordination.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — B: Orchestrated SAGA with Outbox

A booking is a long-running, multi-service business process that crosses regions and includes an external side effect (charging a card). The right model is a saga: a sequence of local ACID transactions, each paired with a compensating action, instead of one global lock. Among the saga options, an orchestrated saga with the outbox pattern and idempotent steps fits best because the stated requirement is a definite outcome for every booking. Here's why it stands out:

  • Availability under failure: No cross-service locks are held. Each service commits locally and moves on, so a slow or unavailable region delays its own step instead of freezing the whole booking path.
  • Definite outcome: The orchestrator persists the saga state after every step, so it always knows whether a booking is mid-flight, confirmed or compensating. Timeouts, retries and terminal states (CONFIRMED or CANCELLED_REFUNDED) are explicit, which is exactly what 'confirmed or definite failure with a refund' demands.
  • Outbox solves the dual-write problem: Each service writes its state change and the outgoing message in one local transaction, and a relay publishes it afterward. No state change is ever committed without its message, and no message is sent for a rolled-back change. Delivery is at-least-once, so every step must be idempotent.
  • Compensations model real business semantics: Releasing a seat hold or voiding or refunding a payment is a semantic undo, not a database rollback. Order the steps so the hardest-to-undo action (capture payment, send email) comes late: hold inventory with a TTL, authorize payment, confirm the reservation, then capture and notify.
  • Observability: One saga record per booking answers 'where is booking X stuck?' with a single lookup. In choreography you reconstruct that answer from scattered events across services.
  • Why not choreography first? With four services and a strict linear flow, choreography spreads the process logic across consumers, makes status queries and timeouts harder, and invites cyclic event dependencies as the flow evolves.

A production-grade saga includes idempotency keys on every command, timeouts with terminal states, retry with backoff, a dead-letter or manual-review queue, and a reconciliation job that compares your records with the payment provider's. Measure saga completion rate, compensation rate and time-in-state from day one.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🔒

A — Distributed 2PC (XA)

2PC is a blocking protocol: if the coordinator fails between prepare and commit, participants stay in-doubt and hold locks. Latency is at least two round trips gated by the slowest participant, which is painful across regions at thousands of requests per second. Payment gateways and notification providers generally don't support XA at all. Caveat: 2PC is acceptable inside a single data center or database cluster with XA-capable resources and short transactions, but that is not this system.

📣

C — Choreographed SAGA

Choreography removes the single coordinator, but the booking logic becomes implicit: spread across consumers, hard to reason about, and hard to answer 'what is the status of this booking?'. Timeouts, ordering and compensation sequencing must be re-implemented in each service, and adding a step risks cyclic dependencies. Caveat: it works well for short flows with two or three loosely coupled participants and no strong need for a single status view.

🗄️

D — Centralized Database / Distributed Locks

Pulling all data into one database destroys service autonomy and creates a global single point of failure that can't be active in multiple regions. It also doesn't cover the side effects that matter most: a database rollback does not un-charge a credit card or unsend an email. Distributed locks on a shared DB add lease-expiry and fencing problems. Caveat: a single database is a legitimate choice for a small system or a modular monolith, where it is a deliberate trade-off rather than an escape from coordination.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — 2PC (XA)

All participants are XA-capable resources inside one trust domain and data center (for example, two databases behind one application), transactions are short, and throughput is modest.

📌

B — Orchestrated SAGA

Multi-step, multi-service business processes with external side effects, a need for a single status and audit view, and requirements for a definite outcome with compensation.

📌

C — Choreographed SAGA

Short flows with few participants, highly autonomous teams, and no requirement for a central status view or complex timeout handling.

📌

D — Central DB / Locks

A small system or modular monolith where services are not truly independent and a single database is an acceptable, deliberate coupling.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Non-idempotent steps and compensations: At-least-once delivery means retries are guaranteed. A duplicate 'reserve' or 'refund' without idempotency keys causes double holds or double refunds.
  • Treating compensation as rollback: A refund is not an undo. Fees, emails and downstream effects may remain. Put non-compensable steps (capture, notify) as late as possible.
  • Dual writes without an outbox: Committing to the database and then publishing to the broker can lose the message on a crash. Write both in one local transaction and relay.
  • No timeouts or terminal states: Sagas that wait forever pile up as 'stuck' bookings. Every state needs a deadline and an exit.
  • God orchestrator: Business rules migrating into the orchestrator couples every service to it. Keep it to sequencing; domain logic stays in the services.
  • Skipping reconciliation: Even correct sagas drift (provider outages, manual fixes). A periodic reconciliation job is the safety net.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Mature booking platforms treat the saga as a first-class domain model, not an afterthought. A typical rollout looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Model the state machine: List states, transitions, timeouts and a compensation for every step before writing code. Decide which steps are compensable and which are not.
  • Step 1 — Orchestrator plus outbox: Persist saga state per booking; every service writes state and outbox message in one local transaction.
  • Step 2 — Idempotency everywhere: Idempotency keys on commands, a processed-messages table in consumers, and the payment provider's idempotency keys on charges and refunds.
  • Step 3 — Timeouts, retries and parking lots: Bounded retries with backoff, then a dead-letter or manual-review queue with alerting.
  • Step 4 — Reconciliation: A scheduled job compares bookings, inventory holds and provider payments, and repairs drift.
  • Step 5 — Observability: Trace by saga ID, dashboards for time-in-state and compensation rate, and runbooks for stuck sagas.

Pick the saga style by process complexity and the need for a single status view, not by fashion.

💡

Key Takeaway

When one business operation spans services, regions and external providers, avoid global locks and coordinate through compensatable local transactions. An orchestrated saga with outbox and idempotent steps gives availability and a definite, auditable outcome. The price is weaker isolation and more engineering around retries, compensation and reconciliation.
