Observability Logs vs Traces Metrics
Challenge

Elena Addressing Production Blind Spots: Logs, Traces, or Metrics?

Elena, a senior backend engineer, owns a microservices payment platform handling tens of thousands of transactions per minute across hundreds of services. Users see sporadic tail-latency spikes and occasional payment failures that are hard to reproduce. Today the system centralizes raw logs, runs a basic metrics pipeline for simple SLIs, and uses a custom tracer that uniformly samples about 1% of traffic. Alerts fire without obvious causes, so on-call engineers dig through huge log volumes. She must improve observability without adding meaningful latency, while controlling cost and helping engineers find the root causes of rare tail failures quickly.

A
Keep synchronous structured logging for every request, sending full request and response payloads from each service to a central log cluster, relying on log queries for troubleshooting and extending retention.
✗ Not Best
B
Enable continuous, unsampled distributed tracing on every RPC, capturing full span payloads and stack traces and sending all traces to a central tracing backend.
✗ Not Best
C
Adopt SLI/SLO-based metrics alerting plus low-overhead basic tracing on all traffic, use adaptive or tail-based sampling to keep full traces for errors and slow requests, and emit structured logs with correlation IDs, forwarded asynchronously by sidecars or agents to a central store with strict retention and redaction.
✓ Best Answer
D
Deploy sidecar agents that asynchronously ship logs and traces at a uniform probabilistic sampling rate (5–10%), keep trace and log data separate, and rely on dashboards and manual trace lookups for root-cause analysis.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: SLO Metrics + Tail-Sampled Traces

The problem is not a lack of data. It is data that is expensive, unlinked and sampled in the wrong way. Tail failures are by definition rare, so a uniform 1% (or even 10%) sample will usually miss exactly the requests you need. The strongest design uses each signal for what it does best: metrics to detect and scope the problem (SLO burn-rate alerts), traces kept by outcome (tail-based sampling retains errors and slow requests) to find where time or failures occur, and correlated structured logs to explain why. Here's why it stands out:

  • Samples the requests that matter: Tail-based sampling decides after a trace completes, so it can keep every trace with an error or high latency, plus a small baseline of normal ones. A 1% uniform sampler keeps about 1% of the slow requests; tail-based sampling keeps nearly all of them.
  • Detects with signals, not guesses: SLI/SLO metrics with multi-window burn-rate alerts tell you that the user experience is degrading and how fast, at low cost and with low alert noise. Histograms show the p99 shape, and exemplars link a latency bucket directly to a trace ID.
  • Low overhead on the request path: Metrics are cheap in-process counters, spans are exported asynchronously, and logs are shipped by agents off the critical path. Nothing synchronous waits on the observability backend.
  • Correlated, so investigation is fast: A shared trace_id in every log line and metric exemplar lets an engineer move from alert, to slow trace, to culprit span, to the exact log lines in minutes instead of grepping terabytes.
  • Cost and compliance control: Keeping full detail only for interesting traces often cuts storage by an order of magnitude or more compared with unsampled tracing and full-payload logging, and redaction keeps card numbers and personal data out of telemetry (critical for PCI scope).

A well-designed pipeline includes W3C Trace Context propagation, RED and SLO burn-rate alerts, latency histograms with exemplars, tail-based sampling in a collector tier, structured logs carrying trace and span IDs, and redaction at the source. Measure how often an SLO-breaching request has a retained trace one click away; if it is not close to 100%, tune the sampling policy.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

📜

A — Full Synchronous Logging

Synchronous logging puts the log backend on the request path, so a slow or unavailable log cluster slows or breaks payments. Full request and response payloads multiply cost and pull card data and personal data into logs, which is a PCI and privacy risk. Uncorrelated logs are also the hardest signal to search for a rare tail event; longer retention just extends the status quo's problem. Caveat: detailed logs are essential for explaining a failure once you know where to look. Keep them structured, asynchronous, redacted and correlated, and sample debug-level volume.

🔬

B — Unsampled Full Tracing

100% tracing gives complete visibility, but at tens of thousands of transactions per minute across hundreds of services the span volume, CPU overhead, network egress and storage cost are enormous, and the tracing backend itself becomes a scaling and reliability concern. Most of that data describes healthy requests nobody will read. Caveat: reasonable for a small system, a short-lived deep dive on one service, or a low-volume pre-production environment.

🎲

D — Uniform 5–10% Sampling

Head-based probabilistic sampling decides before the outcome is known, so it keeps only 5–10% of the slow or failed requests too. Rare tail failures often have no retained trace at all, and cost still scales linearly with traffic. Keeping logs and traces unlinked and relying on manual lookups means every investigation starts from scratch. Caveat: a modest uniform sample remains useful as the baseline inside a tail-based policy, for capacity and dependency analysis.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Full Synchronous Logging

Small systems, or audit trails and forensic needs for specific regulated events (with redaction), rather than as the main tool for diagnosing latency.

📌

B — Unsampled Full Tracing

Low-traffic services, staging environments, or short, targeted deep-dive sessions on a single service or route.

📌

C — SLO Metrics + Tail Sampling

High-volume, many-service production systems where tail failures matter and cost, latency and compliance must be controlled. The default for payment platforms.

📌

D — Uniform 5–10% Sampling

Systems where failures are common and evenly distributed, or as the baseline component inside a tail-sampling policy.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Averaging latency: Means and averaged percentiles hide the tail. Use histograms and aggregate buckets (not pre-computed p99s) so percentiles stay valid across instances.
  • Head-sampling for tail problems: Deciding at the start of a request cannot know it will be slow. Use tail-based sampling (optionally with a small head-based baseline) for error and latency analysis.
  • Tail sampling without trace-ID routing: If spans of one trace land on different collectors, none can decide correctly and traces break. Use a load-balancing exporter keyed by trace ID.
  • Logging sensitive data: Card numbers, tokens and personal data in logs create compliance exposure. Redact at the source and never log full payment payloads.
  • High-cardinality metric labels: User or order IDs as labels explode the number of series. Keep those in traces and logs, and use exemplars to link.
  • Alerting on causes instead of symptoms: CPU and queue-depth alerts create noise. Alert on SLO burn rate and use traces to find the cause.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, observability programs mature in layers: detect cheaply, retain selectively, correlate everything. A typical sequence looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Define SLIs and SLOs: For payments, pick availability and latency SLIs (successful authorizations, p99 latency against a target) and alert on multi-window burn rates rather than raw thresholds.
  • Step 1 — Standardize instrumentation: Adopt OpenTelemetry SDKs everywhere, propagate W3C Trace Context, emit RED metrics with latency histograms, and attach exemplars.
  • Step 2 — Correlate the logs: Switch to structured JSON logs that always include trace_id, span_id, service and version, shipped asynchronously by agents with backpressure and drop policies.
  • Step 3 — Deploy a tail-sampling tier: Agents forward spans to a collector tier load-balanced by trace ID; policies keep errors, slow traces and critical routes plus a small baseline.
  • Step 4 — Protect data and cost: Redact PAN, tokens and personal data at the source, set per-signal retention (short for debug logs, longer for aggregates), and budget metric cardinality.
  • Step 5 — Close the loop: Build dashboards with exemplar links and runbooks that start at an alert and end at a trace, then use postmortems to refine sampling policies and alerts.

Measure whether the next incident was explained in minutes; that is the real test.

💡

Key Takeaway

Detect with metrics, explain with outcome-sampled traces, and confirm with correlated logs. Tail-based sampling keeps the rare slow and failed requests that uniform sampling throws away, asynchronous shipping keeps the request path fast, and redaction plus retention controls keep cost and risk in check.
