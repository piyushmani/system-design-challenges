When a region goes dark: choosing the write strategy for chaos experiments

Ethan, an architect for a worldwide SaaS backend, plans chaos experiments that simulate partial network partitions and full region failures. The application accepts user-facing, non-financial writes (profile updates, settings) from clients in every region. The business goals are low write latency and high availability during network splits, minimal data loss, and state reconciliation that is observable and manageable. He must choose the write-handling architecture that gives the best compromise between availability, latency, consistency and observability.

A
Active-active multi-region setup: permit writes in any region and resolve synchronization conflicts using deterministic rules or CRDTs. Strive for high availability and minimal latency by making all data types conflict-free.
✗ Not Best
B
Single primary with synchronous cross-region replication (quorum): direct all write operations to a primary region and synchronously replicate them to secondary regions to ensure strong consistency, falling back on failover during complete outages.
✗ Not Best
C
Local durable writes with async replication: locally acknowledge and log writes in the isolated region (e.g., using a local write-ahead log or Kafka), and asynchronously sync/merge them to the global dataset, utilizing idempotency, reconciliation, and robust conflict observability.
✓ Best Answer
D
Global traffic routing with strict write rejection: identify regional issues and steer writes to healthy regions; if a user's primary region is down, refuse writes (HTTP 503) to maintain a single authoritative shard and prevent background reconciliation efforts.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: Local Durable Writes with Async Replication and Observable Reconciliation

For non-financial, user-scoped data where the stated priorities are availability, low latency and manageable reconciliation, the right trade is to choose availability over cross-region consistency during a partition (the AP side of CAP) and to make the resulting divergence safe, bounded and visible. Option C does exactly that: the write is acknowledged after it is durable in the local region, replication happens in the background, and idempotency plus a reconciliation pipeline make the merge deterministic and measurable. It is also the only option that treats observability of reconciliation as a first-class requirement, which is precisely what a chaos experiment needs to verify. Here's why it stands out:

  • No cross-region hop on the write path: the acknowledgement depends only on the local durable log (replicated across availability zones inside the region). Write latency stays at local-region levels, and an isolated region keeps accepting writes while the partition lasts.
  • Bounded data loss: an acknowledged write is already durable locally, so a partition alone loses nothing. The only exposure is the unreplicated tail if an entire region is destroyed before it drains, and that tail is measurable as replication lag (your effective RPO), so you can alert on it.
  • Idempotency makes retries and replays safe: every command carries a request id and the entity version it was based on. Duplicated, reordered or replayed deliveries after the partition heals converge to the same state instead of double-applying.
  • Reconciliation is observable by design: the log is an auditable history, and replication lag, conflict count, reconciliation queue depth and time-to-convergence become explicit metrics and dashboards. Option A hides divergence inside merge functions; option C surfaces it.
  • It is a superset of option A: you can still use CRDTs or per-field last-writer-wins where the data genuinely merges (counters, sets, toggles), while adding durability, idempotency and a conflict log for fields that do not. That is why C is stronger than A, not merely different from it.

A robust implementation has a durable local log, idempotency keys, versioned or HLC-stamped updates, per-field merge policies, throttled replay, and anti-entropy repair that compares replicas and fixes drift. Treat replication lag, conflict rate and convergence time as SLOs, and let the chaos experiments prove they hold.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🔀

A — Active-Active with CRDTs for Everything

Active-active is the right replication topology, and CRDTs are a good tool, but the option's key assumption, making all data types conflict-free, does not hold for real profile data. Two concurrent renames of a display name have no semantic merge; a CRDT only guarantees convergence (typically by silently keeping one value), not preservation of user intent. The option also says nothing about durability of acknowledged writes or about how divergence is surfaced, which are two of Ethan's explicit goals. Caveat: for genuinely mergeable data (counters, sets, flags) CRDTs are excellent, and they fit inside option C.

🔒

B — Single Primary with Synchronous Quorum Replication

This gives strong consistency and near-zero RPO, but it pays for them in exactly the dimensions Ethan prioritises. Every write from a distant region pays a cross-region round trip, and during a partition the side that cannot reach the quorum or the primary is blocked or failing. Failover on a full region loss works, but it is slow compared with simply continuing to accept writes locally. Caveat: this is the correct choice for the financial or invariant-bound data that Ethan's scenario explicitly excludes.

🚫

D — Strict Write Rejection (HTTP 503)

This is the simplest model to reason about, since there is one authoritative copy and nothing to reconcile, but it deliberately gives up availability for every user whose home region is impaired. The option also contradicts itself: it says to steer writes to healthy regions, then to refuse them when the home region is down. In practice, in-flight writes, client retries and failover state still need handling, so 'no reconciliation' is not really achievable, and a wave of 503s tends to trigger client retry storms. Caveat: steering writes to a healthy region is a useful mitigation, but refusing them is the opposite of the stated availability goal.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Active-Active + CRDTs

The data is naturally mergeable (counters, sets, presence, collaborative structures), you accept silent convergence, and you have the expertise to model it. Often used as a component inside a larger design.

📌

B — Single Primary + Sync Quorum

Financial, inventory or uniqueness-bound data where a lost or conflicting write is unacceptable and the business accepts higher latency and reduced availability during partitions.

📌

C — Local Durable + Async Replication

User-scoped, non-financial data in a multi-region SaaS where availability and latency matter most, some staleness is acceptable, and reconciliation must be visible and controllable.

📌

D — Strict Write Rejection

Short, planned degradation modes for data that must have a single authority, or as a temporary safety valve for a specific entity class while a regional failover completes.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Acknowledging before the write is durable: answering from memory and flushing later turns every crash into silent data loss. Ack only after the local log append is replicated within the region.
  • Wall-clock last-write-wins: a region with a fast clock wins every conflict. Use HLCs or version vectors and log each resolution.
  • No idempotency keys: client retries and replays after healing create duplicate side effects. Every command needs a request id that the apply path deduplicates.
  • Treating all data the same: applying this model to unique usernames or entitlements produces duplicates that cannot be repaired later. Classify first.
  • Unthrottled replay after heal: a backlog flood can take down the region that just recovered. Rate-limit and prioritise.
  • No convergence check: without anti-entropy comparisons (checksums or Merkle trees) a lost replication message can leave replicas permanently different.
  • Testing only clean, symmetric failures: real outages are partial and asymmetric (A reaches B but B cannot reach A). Include those, plus clock skew, in the experiment plan.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Real teams treat this as a data-classification and experiment-design problem, not just a pattern choice. A typical path looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Classify data by consistency need: settings and profile fields can diverge safely; uniqueness constraints, money and entitlements cannot. Only the first class goes through this design.
  • Step 1 — Define steady-state SLOs for the experiment: write success rate, p99 write latency, replication lag, conflict rate and time-to-convergence. These are the hypotheses the chaos tests will confirm or refute.
  • Step 2 — Build the write path: local durable log, request-id idempotency, entity version or HLC on every command, and a replicator with checkpoints and backoff.
  • Step 3 — Decide the merge policy per field: mergeable (union, max, counters), deterministic winner (HLC then region id) with a conflict log, or escalate to the user or to a reviewer for sensitive fields.
  • Step 4 — Run the experiments: asymmetric and partial partitions (the nastiest case), full region blackhole, clock skew, duplicate and reordered delivery, and healing with a very large backlog. Tools such as Toxiproxy, tc/netem, Chaos Mesh, Gremlin or a cloud fault-injection service can inject these.
  • Step 5 — Operationalise: conflict dashboards, replay throttles, runbooks and recurring game days so the behaviour stays proven as the system changes.

Prove convergence and bounded loss with experiments, not with diagrams.

💡

Key Takeaway

When writes are non-financial and availability and latency matter most, acknowledge locally and durably, replicate asynchronously, and make reconciliation idempotent and observable. Pick merge rules per field, never rely on wall-clock last-write-wins, and keep invariant-bound data on a strongly consistent path. The chaos experiments should measure replication lag, conflict rate and convergence time, not just whether the service stayed up.
