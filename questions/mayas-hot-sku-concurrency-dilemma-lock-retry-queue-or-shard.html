Maya’s Hot-SKU Concurrency Dilemma: Lock, Retry, Queue, or Shard?

Maya owns the inventory service of an international e-commerce platform. During flash sales, 5k–20k requests per second hit a handful of hot SKUs, each with only a limited number of units (hundreds to a few thousand), so demand exceeds supply by orders of magnitude. Checkout has a 100 ms latency budget and overselling is strictly prohibited. The first prototype used SELECT ... FOR UPDATE in a transaction, which serialized all requests on the same row, capped throughput and blew up tail latency at peak. She must pick a new concurrency design that balances scalability, availability, latency, consistency, observability and fault tolerance.

A
Maintain synchronous updates in the central relational database, but employ pessimistic locking (SELECT FOR UPDATE) alongside connection-pool optimization. Depend on upgrading database hardware and utilizing read replicas to manage the read traffic.
✗ Not Best
B
Transition to optimistic concurrency control: introduce a version/CAS column on the inventory records, perform read-modify-write operations using conditional UPDATE statements (WHERE version = x), and retry when conflicts occur. Implement short-lived read caches to minimize database load.
✗ Not Best
C
Shift to asynchronous inventory updates: rapidly accept orders, place change events into a queue (like Kafka or RabbitMQ), and use consumers to process inventory modifications and perform reconciliation. Display availability as eventually consistent and present a "pending" status to users until final confirmation is achieved.
✗ Not Best
D
Implement a per-SKU single-writer or sharded architecture: direct all write operations for a specific SKU to its designated shard or leader (such as an actor or a solitary consumer for a partition) which serializes updates in memory and writes to a durable log (or uses write-behind to the DB). Utilize leader election and replicas to handle failovers, while keeping reads cached or serving them from the saved state.
✓ Best Answer
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — D: Per-SKU Single Writer

The problem is write contention on one logical counter. Locks and retries try to manage that contention inside the database, while a single writer per SKU removes it: one owner applies decrements one after another in memory, with no lock waits and no network round trips inside the critical section. Safety comes from one owner at a time plus a durable, replicated log, not from row locks. Here's why it stands out:

  • Removes the bottleneck instead of tuning it: A row lock held across a network round trip and commit (say 2–5 ms) caps one row at a few hundred updates per second. A single owner thread doing in-memory check-and-decrement handles hundreds of thousands per second on one core.
  • Correct by construction: With exactly one active owner per SKU, “check available ≥ qty, then decrement” is trivially atomic. Overselling is impossible as long as ownership is fenced and the reservation is acknowledged only after it is durably logged.
  • Predictable tail latency: Requests wait in a bounded in-memory queue instead of a lock-wait queue holding DB connections. Once stock hits zero the owner answers “sold out” from memory, so the 5k–20k requests per second that arrive after sell-out become nearly free.
  • Scales out: SKUs are hashed to shards, and a single extreme SKU can be split into N sub-buckets (each owning a slice of its stock), the standard sharded-counter technique.
  • Fault tolerant: The log is replicated (Kafka partition with min.insync.replicas, a Raft group, or Redis with AOF and a replica). After a failure a new leader replays the log or a snapshot, and the database receives changes through an asynchronous projection for reporting and reconciliation.

Use reserve-then-confirm holds with a TTL, make every request idempotent by order ID, put a waiting room or rate limiter in front, and verify the invariant sold + available ≤ initial stock continuously. Load-test by killing the leader mid-sale.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🔒

A — Pessimistic Locking + Bigger Hardware

Throughput on a single row is roughly 1 / lock-hold-time no matter how large the server is. Extra connections only lengthen the queue, and every waiting request holds a pooled connection, so the pool exhausts and other SKUs and checkouts are dragged down too, with lock timeouts and deadlocks at the tail. Read replicas help reads, not the contended write. Caveat: it is perfectly fine for normal SKUs with low contention, so keep it as the default for the long tail.

🔁

B — Optimistic Concurrency (Version / CAS)

No locks are held, but with 20k requests per second on one row, only one writer per version wins and the rest fail and retry. That means wasted work, retry storms and rising tail latency, while total throughput is still capped by how fast that single row can commit. Caching helps reads, not the contended write. A conditional decrement (SET qty = qty - 1 WHERE qty >= 1) avoids read-modify-write retries but still serializes on the row lock. Caveat: great for low-to-moderate contention.

📨

C — Asynchronous Updates with a Queue

“Accept first, decide later” means the system cannot truthfully confirm an order without knowing stock exists. Accepting more orders than units is overselling, followed by cancellations that hurt trust, and this directly violates the “strictly prohibited” requirement. Queue lag during the spike also stretches the “pending” experience. A queue is useful inside option D (as the per-SKU serialized log), but the reservation decision must happen before the confirmation. Caveat: fine for pre-order or backorder models where overselling is acceptable.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Pessimistic Locking

Low-contention workloads (regular SKUs) where simplicity and strict correctness matter more than peak throughput.

📌

B — Optimistic CAS

Moderate contention (hundreds of requests per second per row) where conflicts are rare enough that retries stay cheap.

📌

C — Async Queue

Backorder, pre-order or ticket-waitlist models where accepting beyond stock is a deliberate, communicated business choice.

📌

D — Per-SKU Single Writer

Extreme contention on a few hot counters (flash sales, ticket drops) with a strict no-oversell rule and tight latency, as in this scenario.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Acknowledging before the log is durable: A crash after the ack but before replication loses reservations. Acknowledge only after a quorum append.
  • No fencing on leader change: A paused old leader can wake up and keep accepting reservations (split brain). Attach an epoch to every log append and reject stale epochs.
  • Reservations without expiry: Abandoned carts hold stock forever. Use TTL holds and a sweeper that releases expired reservations.
  • Non-idempotent requests: A client retry reserves twice. Deduplicate by order ID inside the owner.
  • One owner for an extreme SKU with no splitting plan: Even in-memory serialization has a ceiling. Be ready to split the stock into sub-buckets.
  • No admission control: Letting 20k requests per second reach the owner wastes capacity on requests that will be rejected. Rate limit or queue at the edge and short-circuit on sold out.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Flash-sale systems separate the hot path from the long tail and protect the core with admission control. A typical sequence:

  • Step 0 — Identify hot SKUs ahead of time: Use the campaign calendar to route flash-sale SKUs to the special path. Everything else stays on ordinary conditional-update transactions.
  • Step 1 — Pre-warm and pool: Load flash-sale stock into the owner before the sale starts, in a separate inventory pool from regular stock.
  • Step 2 — Admission control: A waiting room or rate limiter at the edge keeps owner load bounded, with a fast “sold out” short-circuit once stock reaches zero.
  • Step 3 — Reserve, then confirm: A reservation with a TTL (for example 10 minutes) is confirmed on payment success and released on failure or expiry, all idempotent by order ID.
  • Step 4 — Durable log + projection: All changes are appended to the replicated log. The database, search and analytics subscribe to it, and a reconciliation job compares log-derived stock with the database.
  • Step 5 — Failure drills and observability: Kill the leader under load, verify the no-oversell invariant and fencing, and watch queue depth, reserve p99 and rejections by reason.

Don't make the database fight itself over one row. Give the hot row a single owner.

💡

Key Takeaway

When many requests contend for one logical counter, locks and retries cannot scale. Serialize ownership instead: one writer per hot SKU, durable replicated log, fenced leader election, TTL reservations and admission control. That keeps latency flat, prevents overselling and degrades safely when a leader fails.
