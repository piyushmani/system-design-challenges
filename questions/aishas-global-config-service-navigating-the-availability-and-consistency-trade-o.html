Challenge

Aisha's global config service: navigating the availability and consistency trade-off

Aisha is building a globally distributed user-settings service. It must meet 99.99% availability for reads and writes, serve typical reads in under 50 ms worldwide, handle a moderate write load (hundreds per second), and never allow conflicting or lost updates (strict consistency on writes). Each cloud region averages about 99.9% uptime and inter-region links can be unstable. Settings are small and read far more often than they are written, and the cluster can span at least three independent regions. She must choose the architecture that meets the SLO, keeps reads fast, and eliminates conflicting or lost writes.

A
A 3-region majority-quorum cluster (Paxos/Raft) where a write commits only after a majority of regions acknowledge it. Reads are served from local replicas, using leader-aware redirects or session tokens to guarantee linearizable reads, with automatic leader election and health checks maintaining quorum availability.
✓ Best Answer
B
A single primary region handling all writes with synchronous replication to a passive standby in another region for failover, while reads go to potentially stale read replicas in various regions.
✗ Not Best
C
Active-active multi-master writes in all regions with asynchronous replication and client-side conflict resolution (CRDTs or last-writer-wins), sending reads and writes to the nearest region.
✗ Not Best
D
Region-local primaries that each accept local writes, replicate asynchronously between regions, and resolve conflicts with last-writer-wins, giving fast local reads and eventual convergence at the cost of possible data loss during regional failover.
✗ Not Best

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — A: Multi-Region Quorum (Raft/Paxos)

The key requirement is strict consistency on writes combined with higher availability than any single region provides. Only consensus-based replication achieves both: with three regions, a write commits when two of three agree, so the system keeps accepting writes after losing any one region or link, while no two regions can ever commit conflicting values. Each region is only 99.9% available, so a design that depends on one specific region (options B and, in effect, D) cannot reach 99.99%, and designs that accept conflicting writes (C and D) fail the correctness requirement outright. Here's why it stands out:

  • The availability math works: If each region is 99.9% available and failures are independent, the chance that at least two of three are up is about 99.9997%, comfortably above the 99.99% target. A single-primary design is capped near 99.9% plus whatever failover quality adds.
  • Conflicts are prevented, not repaired: A consensus log gives every update a single total order, so two clients cannot both 'win' with different values. Add a version or compare-and-set precondition on writes to turn lost updates into explicit, retryable failures.
  • Write cost fits the load: A commit costs roughly one round trip to the nearest majority of regions, commonly tens to a couple of hundred milliseconds depending on geography. At hundreds of writes per second this is fine, and it is only paid on writes.
  • Fast reads where it matters: Most reads are served from the local replica with a session token (the client presents the last commit version it saw, and the replica answers only once it has applied that version). That gives read-your-writes and monotonic reads at local latency. A fully linearizable read costs a check with the leader (a read-index round trip) or a leader lease, so reserve it for calls that explicitly need it.
  • Automatic failover without split brain: Leader election requires a majority, so a partitioned minority cannot elect a second leader or accept writes.
  • Proven at scale: Google Spanner and CockroachDB replicate across regions with Paxos/Raft-style consensus, and etcd and Consul use Raft for configuration-style data. The Raft paper (Ongaro and Ousterhout, 2014) and its follow-up thesis describe the read-index and lease-read approaches to linearizable reads.

A well-run deployment uses 3 or 5 voters across independent regions, version-checked (compare-and-set) writes, session-token local reads with an explicit linearizable option, leader placement near the write hot spots, and client caches with a last-known-good fallback. Track commit latency, leader elections, replication lag, quorum health, and the fraction of reads served locally; test with game days that kill a region and partition links.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🏢

B — Single Primary + Passive Standby

One writable region caps availability near that region's own 99.9%, and failover to a passive standby takes time and, without a consensus-based election, risks split brain (two primaries accepting writes). Reads from stale replicas also contradict the strict-consistency requirement, and users far from the primary pay a cross-region round trip on every write. Caveat: for a regional or lower-SLO product where an occasional minutes-long write outage is acceptable, this is much simpler and cheaper to operate.

🔀

C — Active-Active + LWW/CRDT

Accepting writes everywhere and reconciling later means conflicts are allowed to happen. Last-writer-wins silently discards one of two concurrent updates (and depends on clock quality), and CRDTs merge values rather than guarantee that a business rule holds. Both violate the 'no conflicting or lost updates' requirement for settings that must be exact. Caveat: CRDTs are an excellent fit for naturally mergeable data (counters, sets, presence), just not for values where only one update may win.

🌍

D — Region-Local Primaries + LWW

This makes the same trade as C with weaker guarantees: asynchronous cross-region replication means acknowledged writes can be lost if a region fails before replicating, and LWW can overwrite newer intent with older. It is attractive for latency but explicitly gives up strict consistency, so it cannot meet the requirements as stated. Caveat: if the requirement were relaxed to 'eventual consistency is fine', this would be a reasonable low-latency choice.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Multi-Region Quorum

Small, high-value, correctness-critical data (configuration, feature flags, account settings, metadata, locks) that needs both strong consistency and survival of a regional outage, with a moderate write rate.

📌

B — Single Primary + Standby

Single-region-centric products or tools with relaxed availability targets, where a short write outage during failover is acceptable and operational simplicity matters more.

📌

C — Active-Active LWW/CRDT

Naturally mergeable data (counters, sets, collaborative text, presence) that needs local-write latency in every region and can tolerate eventual convergence.

📌

D — Region-Local Primaries + LWW

High-volume, low-stakes data (activity, caches, telemetry) where occasional lost or overwritten updates are acceptable and local latency matters most.

🧑‍💻 Version-Checked Writes and Session-Token Reads

Writes carry an expected version so conflicting updates fail explicitly; reads use a session token for local, read-your-writes access, with a leader read only when required and a last-known-good fallback when the majority is unreachable:

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming linearizable reads are free: Reading from any follower can return stale data. Use read-index, leader leases, or session tokens, and measure the latency of each.
  • Witness or tie-breaker that is not independent: Quorum needs independent failure domains; a witness in the same provider zone as a voter defeats the purpose.
  • Ignoring correlated failures: Multiplying 99.9% three times assumes independence; shared deployments, DNS, and control planes break that assumption.
  • Writes without preconditions: Two clients can still overwrite each other in sequence. Require a version (compare-and-set) on updates so stale writers get a clear conflict error.
  • Leader far from the write traffic: Every write then pays an extra long-haul hop. Place or move leaders near the heaviest writers.
  • No client-side fallback: If the SDK has no last-known-good cache, a quorum outage becomes a full outage for every dependent service.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, teams state the consistency contract per operation and choose the cheapest mechanism that meets it:

  • Step 0 — Classify operations: Which writes must never conflict? Which reads must be fresh, and which only need read-your-writes? Most settings reads are the latter.
  • Step 1 — Consensus core: Run a 3-region (or 5-region) Raft/Paxos group, or use a managed database that provides it, for the authoritative copy.
  • Step 2 — Fast reads: Serve reads from local replicas with session tokens; offer a linearizable read option for the rare calls that need it.
  • Step 3 — Client and edge caching: Cache with versions and push invalidation (watch streams), and keep a last-known-good copy so a client survives a quorum outage.
  • Step 4 — Define the SLO honestly: Specify what happens for clients stuck in a minority partition (read-only with possibly stale data, or explicit errors) and test it.
  • Step 5 — Chaos testing: Regularly fail a region and partition links to verify leader election time, write availability, and data safety.

Strong consistency and high availability coexist only up to what a majority can reach; be explicit about that boundary.

💡

Key Takeaway

When the requirement is no conflicting or lost updates plus availability above a single region, use majority-quorum consensus across three or more independent regions. Serve most reads locally with session tokens, spend a cross-region round trip only on writes and on reads that truly need linearizability, and be explicit about behavior for clients cut off from the majority.
