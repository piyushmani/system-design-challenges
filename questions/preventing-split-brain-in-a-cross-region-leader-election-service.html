Challenge

Preventing Split-Brain in a Cross-Region Leader Election Service

Lina, a lead backend engineer, is building a global coordination service that manages leader leases for highly active metadata such as feature flags and cluster membership. It must provide low-latency elections and smooth failovers across two main regions. During a network partition it must prevent split-brain (two nodes acting as leader at once) while staying highly available for users in the primary region. With freedom to change the replication and election design, which strategy should she use to avoid split-brain while optimizing for availability and latency?

A
Deploy a consensus algorithm (like Paxos or Raft) with voting nodes distributed across both regions, requiring a majority quorum across data centers. Leaders are only elected when a majority is accessible, ensuring strict consistency and eliminating split-brain, though this introduces higher election latency and potential downtime during inter-region network degradation.
✗ Not Best
B
Assign a local master to each region to accept write operations, utilizing asynchronous cross-region replication. Detect conflicts after the fact and resolve them using last-write-wins or CRDTs. This approach ensures maximum local availability and minimal latency, but introduces the risk of data divergence and complicated conflict resolution following a partition.
✗ Not Best
C
Implement a lease-based leader election system utilizing short, renewable leases along with fencing tokens and a compact, fast quorum of controller nodes situated in the preferred region. Use an external, monotonically increasing tie-breaker (such as a sequence service or a simple atomic store) for definitive arbitration during edge cases, combining health checks with fencing to guarantee an old leader cannot operate after losing its lease.
✓ Best Answer
D
Offload leader arbitration entirely to a managed third-party centralized coordinator that synchronously dictates the leader. Mandate global synchronous replication for all writes; if the central coordinator becomes unreachable, force the cluster into a read-only state to prevent split-brain. This concentrates correctness logic but potentially increases latency and introduces a single point of failure.
✗ Not Best

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: Fenced Leases on a Local Quorum

Split-brain is only harmful if two nodes can both act as leader, so safety has to be enforced at the resources the leader writes to, not just at election time. A short renewable lease granted by a small, linearizable quorum in the primary region gives local-latency elections and renewals. A monotonically increasing fencing token (epoch) checked by every protected resource makes a deposed leader harmless even if it was paused, partitioned or has a skewed clock. Consensus stays inside one region instead of spanning a WAN, which is what delivers both low latency and primary-region availability. Here's why it stands out:

  • Local-latency elections and renewals: With the controller quorum in the preferred region, lease grants and renewals take a few milliseconds, and failover time is roughly the lease TTL (seconds) rather than a WAN election timeout.
  • Safety that does not depend on clocks or pauses: A stale leader that wakes up after a garbage-collection pause or VM stall still carries an old epoch, and the resource rejects it. Leases alone cannot give this guarantee, because a leader can believe it still holds a lease it has already lost.
  • Primary region stays available during a partition: The majority of controllers is local, so the primary side keeps electing and renewing. The other region cannot assemble a quorum, so it cannot promote its own leader.
  • Definitive arbitration for edge cases: A linearizable, monotonically increasing epoch source gives every election a unique number, so simultaneous candidates and ambiguous health checks are settled by comparing epochs rather than by timing.
  • Why not a quorum that spans both regions (option A)? Every election and commit then pays the WAN round trip, and with only two regions no split gives both sides fault tolerance. Option C keeps consensus local and gets split-brain protection from fencing at the resource.

A well-designed service includes leases renewed at about a third of the TTL, epoch fencing enforced on every write path, self-fencing leaders, a replicated linearizable arbiter, a written plan for losing the primary region (witness or manual promotion), and chaos tests that pause leaders, partition regions and skew clocks. Track leader changes per hour, lease renewal latency, time without a leader and fenced-write rejections. A non-zero rejection count means the fence is saving you. If flaps or rejections rise, tune TTLs and health checks before changing topology.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🗳️

A — WAN-Spanning Paxos or Raft

This is safe by construction, but every election and commit crosses regions, and elections can time out when the inter-region link degrades. With two regions, any voter split leaves one side as a single point of failure: lose it and the cluster halts. Caveat: with three regions, or a small witness in a third site, a WAN-spanning consensus group is a strong and simple choice. Option C also uses consensus internally, just kept local.

🧬

B — Local Masters with Last-Write-Wins or CRDTs

This is split-brain by design: both regions accept writes as leader during a partition. Last-write-wins silently discards updates and depends on clocks, and CRDTs can merge data such as counters or sets but cannot merge the idea of a single leader. It suits data that is meant to be writable everywhere, not leadership or lease state.

🏢

D — Third-Party Central Coordinator with Global Sync Replication

A single external coordinator is a single point of failure and a black box for correctness, global synchronous replication adds WAN latency to every write, and forcing read-only mode whenever the coordinator is unreachable turns a coordinator problem into an outage for the primary region too. Outsourcing arbitration also does not remove the need for fencing at the resources.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — WAN-Spanning Consensus

Three or more regions (or a cheap third-site witness), strict linearizability, moderate write rates, and tolerance for WAN round trips.

📌

B — Local Masters + LWW/CRDT

Data (not leadership) that can merge, such as counters, sets or presence, and must stay writable in every region during partitions.

📌

C — Fenced Leases on Local Quorum

Latency-sensitive coordination with a clearly preferred region, a need for primary-region availability, and acceptance of stale or read-only service in the secondary region during partitions.

📌

D — Central Coordinator

Small systems already standardized on a managed lock or coordination service where global write latency is not critical.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Leases without fencing: A paused or partitioned old leader can keep writing after its lease expires. Every protected resource must reject stale epochs.
  • Trusting wall-clock time: Clocks jump and drift. Measure local lease deadlines with a monotonic clock, count from the request start, subtract a drift margin, and let the quorum, not timestamps, order epochs.
  • Using a single-node lock or an unfenced lock service for safety-critical leadership: A lock without a fencing token cannot protect against pauses. Use a consensus-backed store and propagate its epoch.
  • Mis-tuned TTLs: Too short causes flapping and false failovers, too long slows recovery. Renew at about a third of the TTL and tune from observed pause and latency data.
  • Two regions with symmetric votes and no tie-breaker: Either both sides stall, or someone forces a promotion by hand and creates two leaders. Add a witness or declare a preferred region with an explicit failover procedure.
  • Clients that cache the leader forever: They keep talking to a deposed leader. Return the epoch in responses, redirect on rejection, and re-resolve on error.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Teams building coordination services define the failure model first and then layer protections so that safety never depends on timing alone:

  • Step 0 — Define what 'leader' protects: List the resources a leader writes to, the failure model (pauses, partitions, clock skew) and the required behavior in each region during a partition.
  • Step 1 — Choose the arbiter: A managed or self-run etcd, ZooKeeper or Consul group of three or five nodes across availability zones in the primary region, plus a witness plan for the second region.
  • Step 2 — Implement leases with epochs: A TTL of about three renewal intervals, jittered retries, and a monotonically increasing epoch returned with every grant.
  • Step 3 — Enforce fencing everywhere: Every write path compares the epoch and rejects older ones, and leaders self-fence on a local monotonic deadline.
  • Step 4 — Write the region-loss runbook: Decide between witness-based automatic failover and manual promotion, and rehearse it.
  • Step 5 — Observe and break it on purpose: Dashboards for leader churn and renewal latency, and Jepsen-style tests with pauses, partitions and clock skew.

Use consensus to hand out unique epochs, and use fencing to make an old leader harmless.

💡

Key Takeaway

Split-brain is prevented by making a deposed leader powerless, not by hoping it notices in time. Grant short leases from a small, linearizable quorum in the primary region, attach a monotonic fencing epoch that every protected resource enforces, and have leaders stop themselves when their lease deadline passes. Plan explicitly for loss of the primary region with a witness or a manual promotion. If you have three regions available, a WAN-spanning consensus group is a simpler and equally safe alternative.
