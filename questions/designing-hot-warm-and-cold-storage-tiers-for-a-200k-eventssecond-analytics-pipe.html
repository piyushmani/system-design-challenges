Designing Hot, Warm and Cold Storage Tiers for a 200K Events/Second Analytics Pipeline

Ravi, a backend engineer, is designing the storage tier for an analytics pipeline ingesting 200,000 events per second. The system needs sub-100 ms queries over the last 24 hours, ad-hoc analytical queries across months (minutes are acceptable), 3-year retention for legal reasons, and a constrained long-term storage budget. Operators also need visibility into replication delay and reprocessing errors. He must pick a hot/warm/cold architecture that best balances latency, cost, availability, consistency and operational simplicity.

A
Deploy a unified datastore for all data tiers: operate a massive search or analytics cluster (such as Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, or ClickHouse) and leverage its native lifecycle management features to migrate indices across hot, warm, and cold nodes within the same cluster, maintaining a single source of truth.
✗ Not Best
B
Implement a polyglot tiered architecture: maintain a highly responsive hot store (like a time-series database or Kafka with hot indices) for recent 24-hour queries, writing events to it synchronously alongside a durable Kafka commit log. Use an asynchronous CDC/ETL process to compact and store partitioned ORC/Parquet files in object storage (e.g., S3) for warm and cold data, serving these via a query engine like Trino or Athena based on data age, while monitoring lag and errors via the commit log.
✓ Best Answer
C
Adopt a synchronous dual-write strategy: configure all producers to write events concurrently to both a hot datastore (for fast reads) and cold object storage (like Parquet files on S3) to guarantee immediate durability and availability of cold data without an asynchronous pipeline, using a metadata index to track cold objects.
✗ Not Best
D
Utilize a decentralized, edge-oriented tiering approach: deploy ephemeral hot caches in each region to guarantee sub-100ms reads, replicate data periodically across regions, and execute resource-intensive aggregation jobs to move combined data into centralized warm/cold storage. Fulfill cross-region queries by aggregating data from regional caches on the fly or falling back to the warm/cold stores.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — B: Polyglot Tiers with a Log-First Pipeline

The two query requirements pull in opposite directions: sub-100 ms on a small, recent window and cheap minutes-scale scans over years. A single engine has to compromise on one of them. A polyglot design lets each tier do one job: a small, fast hot store for the last day, a durable Kafka log as the replayable system of record for ingest, and columnar files on object storage queried by a separate engine for everything older. A back-of-envelope check shows why: 200,000 events/s is about 17.3 billion events per day, which at an assumed ~500 bytes per event is roughly 8.6 TB/day raw and about 9.5 PB over 3 years before compression. That is feasible on compressed Parquet in object storage and prohibitively expensive on replicated SSD cluster nodes. Here's why it stands out:

  • Sub-100 ms where it matters: The hot tier only holds roughly 24 to 48 hours of data (low single-digit TB after columnar compression, under the same assumptions), small enough to live on NVMe and in cache. Query latency stays flat as history grows, because history lives elsewhere.
  • Cost matches access pattern: Months and years of data sit in compressed columnar files on object storage, with lifecycle rules moving older data to cheaper storage classes. Compute is separate and scales (or is billed per query) only when someone actually runs a historical query.
  • Workload isolation: Months-long ad-hoc scans run on Trino or Athena against object storage, so they cannot starve ingestion or the hot-path queries. In a single cluster, those workloads share CPU, memory, disk and failure domains.
  • A replayable log gives the observability and recovery the operators asked for: Consumer lag on Kafka is a direct measure of replication delay, dead-letter topics capture reprocessing errors, and retention of days allows rebuilding the hot store or re-running ETL after a bug fix.
  • Why not one cluster with lifecycle policies (option A)? It is simpler to operate at small scale, but it couples ingest, hot queries and historical scans into one failure domain, and holding three years on replicated cluster storage is expensive. S3-backed or searchable-snapshot tiers narrow the gap but do not give you independent compute or a replay log.

A well-designed tiering includes Kafka retention longer than your ETL SLA plus a replay window, idempotent event IDs, event-time partitioning, atomic table commits with compaction, a hot-tier window deliberately longer than ETL lag (for example 48 hours of hot data for a 24-hour SLA), a query router that respects a committed watermark, and lifecycle and retention locks on the archive. Track hot-tier p99 latency, consumer lag, ETL freshness, dead-letter rate and cost per TB-month per tier. If hot p99 climbs, shrink the hot window or pre-aggregate common queries.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🗄️

A — Unified Cluster with Lifecycle Tiers

One engine and one source of truth is operationally attractive, and modern engines can push cold data to object storage. But ingest, hot queries and months-long scans still share one cluster, so a heavy analytical query can hurt the 100 ms SLA, and there is no independent replay log for reprocessing. Keeping three years on replicated cluster nodes also costs far more than columnar files on object storage. Caveat: at lower volumes (tens of thousands of events per second or less) or shorter retention, this is a sensible, simpler choice.

🔀

C — Synchronous Dual-Write to Hot and Cold

There is no atomic commit across two stores, so a failure between the two writes leaves the tiers permanently inconsistent. Writing to object storage per event (or per tiny batch) creates a huge number of small files, which makes cold queries slow and expensive, and producers inherit the tail latency of the slowest store. Without a durable log there is also nothing to replay when something goes wrong.

🌍

D — Decentralized Edge Tiering

Nothing in the requirements asks for multi-region reads. Ephemeral regional caches lose data on restart, periodic cross-region replication adds lag and inconsistency, and aggregating across regions on the fly increases latency and cost for exactly the queries that should be simple. It is worth considering only when users and data are genuinely regional with data-residency rules.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Unified Cluster

Moderate volume or short retention, a small team, and tolerance for one shared failure domain. The simplest option when the scale does not justify multiple systems.

📌

B — Polyglot Tiers

High-volume ingest with a strict hot-query SLA, long and cheap retention, independent scaling of storage and compute, and a need to replay or rebuild tiers.

📌

C — Synchronous Dual-Write

Low-volume pipelines where the archive must be durable before acknowledgement, and writes can go through a single batching writer so small files stay manageable.

📌

D — Edge Tiering

Genuinely multi-region data with residency rules or local-latency needs, where regional queries dominate and cross-region queries are rare.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Dual-writing from producers: Writing to two stores without an atomic commit lets them drift apart silently. Write once to a log and derive every tier from it.
  • No watermark at the tier boundary: Without a committed watermark, queries spanning hot and cold data double count the overlap or miss a gap. Route by watermark and keep the hot window longer than ETL lag.
  • The small-files problem: Writing thousands of tiny Parquet files makes cold queries slow and costly. Batch writes and run compaction on a schedule.
  • Partitioning cold data badly: Partition by event date (and a low-cardinality key if needed), not by a high-cardinality field that creates millions of partitions or by ingestion time that scatters late events.
  • Hot retention equal to ETL lag: If the hot tier drops data exactly when ETL finishes, any ETL delay opens a gap. Keep a deliberate buffer.
  • No reconciliation: Without periodic row-count and checksum comparison between tiers, silent loss goes unnoticed, which is a real risk when the data is under legal retention.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Teams building tiered analytics storage profile the workload first and then build the pipeline in layers:

  • Step 0 — Profile the workload: Event size and cardinality, which 24-hour queries truly need under 100 ms (and whether pre-aggregates would serve them), late-arrival distribution, and the exact retention and deletion rules.
  • Step 1 — Kafka as the ingest system of record: Choose partition keys, set retention above the ETL SLA plus a replay window, and add a dead-letter topic.
  • Step 2 — Hot tier: Pick an engine suited to low-latency aggregation (ClickHouse, Druid, Pinot or a time-series database), size it for 24 to 48 hours, and feed it by consuming from Kafka.
  • Step 3 — Cold tier: Stream ETL (Flink, Spark or Kafka Connect) writes Parquet in an Iceberg-style table partitioned by event date, with scheduled compaction, queried through Trino or Athena.
  • Step 4 — Retention and governance: Storage-class lifecycle rules, object lock for legal hold, a deletion workflow for privacy requests, and access controls.
  • Step 5 — Observability and reconciliation: Dashboards for consumer lag, ETL freshness and dead-letter rate, plus hourly row-count reconciliation between Kafka, hot and cold tiers.

Keep one replayable log as the source of truth and let every tier be a derived, rebuildable view.

💡

Key Takeaway

Match each tier to its access pattern: a small, fast hot store for the last day, columnar files on object storage for months and years, and a durable Kafka log feeding both so any tier can be rebuilt and replication delay is directly measurable. Be careful at the tier boundary (watermarks, idempotency, compaction) and avoid synchronous dual writes. If your volume or retention is small, a single engine with lifecycle tiers may be the simpler right answer.
