Challenge

Evolving an E-Commerce Monolith: Modular Monolith vs. Microservices Rewrite

Liam is the backend lead for a fast-growing e-commerce company whose monolith (catalog, payments, inventory, checkout, analytics) runs on one relational database. During flash sales it suffered severe checkout delays, deployment-triggered outages, and overselling caused by delayed inventory updates. He has an 8-person team that must keep shipping features, so he needs a one-year evolution plan that improves availability and scalability without long downtime or hard-to-reason-about consistency problems. The plan must respect strict checkout latency needs and guarantee that stock is never oversold.

A
Do a big-bang rewrite into microservices: split every domain (checkout, catalog, payments, inventory, analytics) into its own service with its own database, use synchronous REST or gRPC between services, and keep cross-service data consistent with two-phase commit or distributed transactions.
✗ Not Best
B
Keep the monolith and scale it up: vertical scaling, read replicas, aggressive caching (application and CDN), query tuning, and heavy CI/CD automation so the monolith absorbs spikes without architectural change.
✗ Not Best
C
Split the monolith into microservices immediately, each with its own database, move to event-driven messaging with sagas and eventual consistency, and invest heavily up front in chaos testing, distributed tracing, and a service mesh.
✗ Not Best
D
Move gradually to a modular monolith: enforce strict domain boundaries and clean interfaces inside the codebase on a shared database for now, extract modules into services only when team scale or bottlenecks require, use async events for non-critical integrations (notifications, analytics), keep strong consistency for checkout and inventory (reservations or optimistic locking), and add feature flags, health checks, and observability for safe incremental releases.
✓ Best Answer
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — D: Modular Monolith With Incremental Extraction

Liam's three symptoms have different root causes, and only one of them is a scaling problem that microservices address directly. Checkout delays come from contention and coupling (analytics, catalog reads, and checkout share one process and one database), deployment outages come from big-bang releases of a tightly coupled codebase, and overselling comes from non-atomic inventory updates, a data-integrity bug that microservices would make harder to fix, not easier. With an 8-person team that must keep delivering features, the best strategy is to create clean seams inside the monolith, fix inventory correctness inside one transactional boundary, and extract services only where evidence justifies the operational cost. Here's why it stands out:

  • Fixes overselling where it belongs: Keeping checkout and inventory in one transactional boundary lets an atomic conditional update or an expiring reservation guarantee stock never goes negative. Splitting them across services would force sagas and compensation onto the single most consistency-sensitive operation in the business.
  • Delivers value early, with low risk: Module boundaries, feature flags, and async events for analytics and notifications can ship in weeks and immediately take load off the checkout path, with no big-bang cutover.
  • Matches the team size: Microservices bring on-call load, service-to-service auth, tracing, per-service pipelines, and data-ownership problems. Eight engineers shipping features cannot absorb that tax across five or more services at once.
  • Extraction becomes cheap when justified: A module that already has a narrow interface, owns its tables, and talks to others through events can be lifted into a service later using the strangler fig approach, with the database split as a late step rather than the first.
  • A proven path: Shopify is a well-known example of running a very large commerce platform as a modular monolith with enforced component boundaries rather than one service per domain.

A solid plan includes atomic inventory reservation with expiry, the transactional outbox pattern for publishing events, idempotent checkout requests, module dependency checks in CI, and read models or replicas for catalog and analytics. Track oversell incidents (target: zero), checkout p95 and p99 during sales, deploy failure rate, and cross-module coupling; extract a service only when a metric shows a boundary problem that a module cannot fix.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

💥

A — Big-Bang Microservices Rewrite

A simultaneous rewrite is the highest-risk option: it slows or freezes feature delivery for months, must rediscover years of hidden business rules, and delivers no value until cutover. Synchronous service calls chain latencies and failures along the checkout path, which can make the delays worse, and two-phase commit or distributed transactions reduce availability and are poorly supported across heterogeneous services. Caveat: a rewrite can make sense for a small, well-understood, low-traffic component with clear requirements; it is a poor fit for a revenue-critical monolith.

⬆️

B — Scale the Monolith Up

Vertical scaling, replicas, caching, and query tuning are valuable tactical moves that should happen immediately, but as a complete strategy they leave the structural problems in place: deploy-triggered outages from coupling, contention between analytics and checkout, and a single database as the ceiling. Caching and replicas also make stale inventory reads more likely, which worsens overselling unless the decrement itself is atomic on the primary. Caveat: if traffic growth is modest and flash sales are rare, this plus a modular cleanup may be enough for a long time.

🕸️

C — Immediate Microservices With Sagas and Mesh

This may be the right end state for some organizations, but it is the wrong first move for an 8-person team. It front-loads a service mesh, tracing, chaos tooling, and per-service data ownership before there is evidence about where the real boundaries are, and eventual consistency on inventory risks overselling unless reservations are modeled very carefully. The usual result is slower feature delivery and a larger operational surface exactly when the business needs stability. Caveat: with many teams, independent scaling needs, and platform engineering capacity, this approach can pay off, ideally reached incrementally.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Big-Bang Microservices Rewrite

Small, well-understood components with clear specifications and low traffic, or an unmaintainable legacy platform where a funded team can run both systems in parallel for months. Rarely right for a revenue-critical monolith.

📌

B — Scale the Monolith Up

Early-stage products with moderate growth where the monolith is healthy, as a tactical first step before any structural change.

📌

C — Immediate Microservices + Sagas

Many independent teams, clearly separated domains, independent scaling needs, and an existing platform team that can run service infrastructure.

📌

D — Modular Monolith

Small to mid-size teams that must keep shipping, need to grow safely, and want to defer distributed-system costs until measured needs justify them.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Modules in name only: Folders without enforced dependency rules let modules reach into each other's tables. Enforce visibility in CI and give each module ownership of its tables.
  • Read-then-write inventory logic: Checking stock and then decrementing in two steps lets concurrent buyers both pass the check. Use an atomic conditional update or a reservation.
  • Using a cached stock number for the decision: A cache or replica can display availability, but the authoritative decrement must happen on the primary.
  • Dual write without an outbox: Writing to the database and publishing an event as two separate steps loses or duplicates events on failure. Use a transactional outbox.
  • Ignoring hot-row contention: A flash-sale SKU turns one row into a global lock. Shard stock counters or add a waiting-room admission gate.
  • Never defining extraction criteria: Without explicit triggers, the modular monolith becomes permanent by default, or services get extracted by fashion.
  • Sharing tables across modules: Shared tables turn a later extraction into a data-migration project. Access another module's data only through its interface.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, teams fix correctness first, then isolate load, then split, with each step independently shippable:

  • Step 0 — Find the real bottlenecks: Profile checkout under flash-sale load, find lock contention and slow queries, and trace oversell incidents to the exact read-then-write race.
  • Step 1 — Fix inventory correctness: Replace read-then-write with an atomic conditional decrement or an expiring reservation, make checkout idempotent, and add a reconciliation job.
  • Step 2 — Take the quick tactical wins: Add read replicas for catalog and reporting, a CDN and cache for catalog pages, and tune the slowest queries (the useful part of Option B).
  • Step 3 — Draw module boundaries: Create modules for catalog, inventory, checkout, payments, and analytics with enforced interfaces and table ownership, plus dependency checks in CI.
  • Step 4 — Decouple non-critical flows: Publish domain events through a transactional outbox to feed notifications, analytics, and search, removing that load from the checkout transaction.
  • Step 5 — Extract by evidence: Peel off the first candidates (analytics, notifications, catalog reads) using the strangler fig approach, keep checkout and inventory together until a measured need forces a split, and use feature flags and canaries throughout.

Treat each extraction as an experiment with a success metric, not as a milestone on a roadmap.

💡

Key Takeaway

Overselling is a data-integrity problem, checkout delay is a contention problem, and outages are a release-coupling problem; none of them requires a rewrite to fix. Build clean module boundaries, keep checkout and inventory strongly consistent in one transactional boundary, move everything non-critical to async events, and extract services only when evidence justifies the added operational cost.
