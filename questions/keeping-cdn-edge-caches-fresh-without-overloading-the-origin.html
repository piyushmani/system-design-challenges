Keeping CDN Edge Caches Fresh Without Overloading the Origin

Noah, a backend architect at a streaming service, uses a global CDN. Video files are mostly immutable, but metadata (titles, thumbnails, availability) changes frequently, and urgent legal takedowns must be reflected at the edge within seconds. The CDN serves billions of requests per day, so both origin load and user latency matter. He needs a freshness strategy that keeps reads fast and hit ratios high, makes invalidation reasonably quick, and avoids a brittle control plane.

A
Apply extremely brief Time-To-Live (TTL) values (e.g., 1 to 5 seconds) or enforce a no-cache policy, requiring every request to be validated against the origin using conditional GETs or ETags. This approach ensures content freshness but significantly increases origin load and introduces extra latency per request.
✗ Not Best
B
Maintain lengthy TTLs to maximize cache hits, while deploying a centralized synchronous purge API. Upon content modification, the origin signals the control plane to issue direct purge commands, waiting for acknowledgment from every Point of Presence (PoP) before declaring success, effectively simulating strong consistency for invalidations.
✗ Not Best
C
Leverage long TTLs in conjunction with cache-control directives like stale-while-revalidate to allow background conditional revalidation at the edge. Supplement this with a best-effort pub/sub invalidation mechanism communicating from the origin to the PoPs (or the CDN's control plane) to expedite propagation. To handle emergency takedowns, incorporate short-lived signed URLs or a specific flag triggering short TTLs.
✓ Best Answer
D
Utilize a multi-CDN strategy paired with cache-busting techniques: avoid cache purging entirely. Instead, deploy updated content using novel content-hash URLs and rotate access keys to execute takedowns. Implement DNS traffic steering alongside origin-initiated redirects during deployments, ensuring edge nodes gradually adopt the updated set of URLs.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — C: Long TTL + Stale-While-Revalidate + Async Invalidation + Takedown Path

The workload isn't one problem, it's three with different freshness needs: immutable video, mutable metadata, and legally urgent takedowns. Option C is the only one that treats them differently: long TTLs with stale-while-revalidate keep hit ratio and latency high, best-effort asynchronous invalidation shortens the freshness window without a brittle control plane, and takedowns get a separate, access-based mechanism (short-lived signed URLs or a no-cache flag) so they never depend on cache purge timing. Here's why it stands out:

  • High hit ratio and low latency: With long s-maxage and stale-while-revalidate, users get an immediate response from the edge while it refreshes in the background. The origin sees roughly one conditional request per object per refresh (further reduced by an origin shield and request collapsing).
  • Fast invalidation without blocking: Purging by surrogate key or tag through the CDN API is asynchronous and idempotent, and on modern CDNs typically propagates within seconds. A slow or unreachable PoP never blocks publishing, and the TTL is the backstop for any missed purge.
  • Takedowns don't depend on cache behavior: Short-lived signed URLs or tokens (for example 60 to 120 seconds) mean revoking access is simply 'stop issuing tokens' and 'edge denies revoked or expired tokens'. The time-to-effect is bounded by the token lifetime even if a purge is delayed. A hard purge runs in parallel as defense in depth.
  • Operationally simple: It uses standard HTTP caching semantics and CDN-native features instead of a custom control plane that must coordinate every PoP.
  • Good fit for the content classes: Immutable video can use content-hashed or versioned URLs with a very long TTL, while metadata gets the SWR plus event-driven purge treatment.

Define explicit SLOs: purge propagation p99, cache hit ratio, stale-served ratio, and takedown time-to-effect. Run takedown drills regularly, because an untested legal path is a liability.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

⏱️

A — Very Short TTL or No-Cache

Freshness is excellent, but the hit ratio collapses: with N PoPs and a TTL of a few seconds, each popular object triggers a revalidation per PoP every few seconds, and every conditional GET still pays an origin round trip. At billions of requests per day this overloads the origin and adds latency for users. Caveat: short TTLs are appropriate for a small set of genuinely volatile objects (for example live availability counters), not as the global policy.

🛑

B — Synchronous Purge with Global Acknowledgment

Waiting for every PoP to acknowledge ties publishing to the slowest or partitioned PoP, which makes the control plane brittle and creates a tail-latency and availability coupling. It also gives a false sense of strong consistency: in-flight requests, client caches and intermediate proxies are outside the guarantee. Caveat: track purge completion for audit and alerting, but do not gate publishing on it.

🔑

D — Multi-CDN with Content-Hash URLs and No Purging

Content-hashed URLs are excellent for immutable assets (and should be used for video and thumbnails), but metadata referenced through stable URLs still needs invalidation. Takedown by rotating keys is blunt: it can break unrelated content under the same key, and DNS steering depends on DNS TTLs, which are not 'seconds'. Multi-CDN also multiplies operational complexity and configuration drift. Caveat: a strong complement to option C for assets, but not a complete answer.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Very Short TTL / No-Cache

A small set of highly volatile, low-volume objects where freshness outweighs cost. Per-user or real-time data is often better served outside the CDN cache.

📌

B — Synchronous Global Purge

Rare, compliance-driven cases with small catalogs where explicit confirmation is required. Even then, run it asynchronously for audit rather than gating publication.

📌

C — Long TTL + SWR + Async Purge

Large catalogs with mixed mutable and immutable content, strict latency goals, and takedown requirements, which is this scenario.

📌

D — Multi-CDN + Hashed URLs

Immutable assets, build artifacts, static sites and video segments with versioned URLs, or when multi-CDN resilience is a business requirement.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using SWR on content that must disappear immediately: Stale-while-revalidate is a deliberate staleness window. Takedown-sensitive objects need no-store or access-based control.
  • Signature or token in the cache key: Every user gets a unique key and a cache miss. Exclude it from the key and validate at the edge.
  • Purging by URL instead of surrogate key: Variants (query strings, formats, languages) are missed. Tag objects and purge by key.
  • Purge storms and thundering herds: A mass purge sends every PoP back to the origin at once. Prefer soft purge with SWR, plus origin shield and request collapsing.
  • No TTL backstop: If you rely only on purge events, one lost message becomes indefinite staleness.
  • Never testing the takedown path: The first real test of the legal SLA should not be a real legal demand.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Large streaming platforms separate content by how it changes, then pick a freshness mechanism per class:

  • Step 0 — Classify content: Immutable assets, mutable metadata, and legally sensitive content each get their own policy.
  • Step 1 — Immutable assets: Content-hashed or versioned URLs with Cache-Control: immutable and a year-long TTL. No invalidation needed.
  • Step 2 — Metadata: Long s-maxage with SWR and stale-if-error, surrogate keys per title, and event-driven soft purge on change.
  • Step 3 — Takedown path: Short-lived signed URLs, a denylist checked at token issue and at the edge, and a hard purge for defense in depth. Audit-log every step.
  • Step 4 — Protect the origin: Origin shield, request collapsing, and rate limits on revalidation after purges.
  • Step 5 — Measure and drill: Dashboards for hit ratio, purge latency and stale-served ratio, plus scheduled takedown drills.

Make freshness a property of the content class, not a global knob.

💡

Key Takeaway

Use long TTLs and stale-while-revalidate for speed, asynchronous purge for freshness, and access control for takedowns. Don't build a control plane that waits for every PoP. The cost is a few more moving parts (event bus, signed URLs, denylist) and a bounded window of staleness for normal metadata.
