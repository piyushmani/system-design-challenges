Maya's Telemetry Bottleneck: Scaling Raw Metrics Without Disrupting Dashboards

Maya is architecting the backend for telemetry from millions of IoT devices, each sending a metric point every 10 seconds (a million devices is already about 100k points/s, and reconnect storms after an outage can multiply that, so the design needs real headroom beyond the stated tens of thousands of writes per second). Dashboards must update in sub-second to a few seconds, analysts need ad-hoc history from days to years, data must be kept for one year with full fidelity for 7 days and downsampled data after that, and the platform must tolerate regional outages. Devices are unreliable, so points arrive late and out of order. She must choose the time-series modeling and ingestion design that best balances scalability, latency, availability and operating cost.

A
Rely on a single strong-consistency relational database (like Postgres) using daily partitioning and synchronous writes. Set up triggers or stored procedures to refresh aggregates during writes. Achieve scalability through vertical scaling and depend on master failover to maintain availability.
✗ Not Best
B
Implement an append-only ingestion pipeline: log device events into a durable message queue (such as Kafka), process streams (using Flink or ksqlDB) to calculate near-real-time aggregates, and write recent data to a specialized TSDB (like TimescaleDB or ClickHouse) for fast dashboards. Store raw events in S3 as time-partitioned Parquet files for extended analytics, and execute offline batch jobs to downsample and compact older records into summary tables.
✓ Best Answer
C
Set up a dedicated time-series database cluster (e.g., InfluxDB, Prometheus remote write, or ClickHouse) as the central data store. Execute synchronous downsampling during ingestion (writing raw data and immediately writing rollups), and utilize node sharding to scale both read and write operations.
✗ Not Best
D
Adopt a wide-column store such as Cassandra utilizing a time-bucketed partition key (e.g., device_id combined with hour/day) to optimize write throughput. Keep materialized views or manage application-level aggregations to serve common queries, accepting eventual consistency for aggregated data.

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — B: Append-Only Stream Pipeline with Hot TSDB and Parquet Lake

This workload has three very different access patterns: a continuous firehose of writes, seconds-fresh dashboards over recent data, and cheap, flexible analytics over long history. Option B gives each pattern its own purpose-built layer, connected by a durable, replayable log. Ingestion never depends on the query stores, dashboards read pre-aggregated hot data, and history lives in cheap, durable, engine-agnostic files. Here's why it stands out:

  • The log decouples everything: Writes land in a replicated Kafka topic (acks=all, spread across AZs), so a TSDB outage or maintenance window never loses points. The log absorbs reconnect storms, and any downstream store can be rebuilt by replay.
  • Near-real-time aggregates: Flink or ksqlDB windowed aggregations (for example 10 s and 1 min) with watermarks and allowed lateness give dashboards ready-made numbers within seconds and handle out-of-order data from flaky devices correctly.
  • The right store per access pattern: A hot TSDB (TimescaleDB/ClickHouse) with a 7-day TTL serves dashboards. Raw events in S3 as Parquet (partitioned by date and hour) serve long-range analysis through Athena, Trino, Spark or ClickHouse, with no lock-in to one engine.
  • Cost shape matches the retention policy: At 100k points/s, 7 days of full fidelity is about 60 billion points. Rolling 10-second data up to 1-minute rows is a 6× reduction and to 1-hour rows a 360× reduction, so a year of summaries is small next to the raw tier.
  • Regional resilience: Kafka replicated across AZs, topic mirroring to a second region, and S3 cross-region replication mean a regional outage leaves dashboards and history available elsewhere. Batch downsampling is deterministic and re-runnable, so late data can be folded in by recomputing a partition.

Treat the pipeline as replayable by design: schema-registered events, idempotent sinks keyed by (device, metric, window), and monitored consumer lag, watermark delay, late-event rate and hot-store query p99. Apply lifecycle rules so raw data leaves the lake on the retention schedule you committed to.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🐘

A — Single Postgres with Triggers

Refreshing aggregates in triggers multiplies the cost of every write and creates row-lock contention on shared aggregate rows. Synchronous commits and one writer put a hard ceiling on throughput, and vertical scaling only postpones it. Seven days of raw points is tens of billions of rows, which strains indexes and vacuum. A single primary also means a regional failure is an outage until failover completes. Caveat: for a fleet of thousands of devices, Postgres with TimescaleDB is a simple and sensible choice.

🗄️

C — Single TSDB Cluster with Synchronous Downsampling

Writing rollups in the ingest path couples ingestion latency and failure to the aggregation logic and amplifies writes. Ingest-time rollups are also wrong for late or out-of-order points unless you can revise them later. There is no durable log to replay if the cluster is damaged, no cheap long-term lake, and the single cluster remains a regional single point of failure. Caveat: a ClickHouse-centric variant with a queue in front and incremental materialized views is a good simplification and is really a lighter version of B.

🧱

D — Cassandra with Time-Bucketed Partitions

Cassandra gives excellent write scaling and multi-DC replication, and bucketing by device and hour avoids unbounded partitions. But ad-hoc analytics (aggregations across devices, years of history) are not what it is built for: no joins, limited aggregation, and wide scans. You end up maintaining aggregates in application code, and Cassandra's materialized views have a long record of production problems (they remain flagged experimental in recent releases). Caveat: it is a strong fit for “latest readings for device X” lookups, paired with an analytical store.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Single Postgres + Triggers

Small fleets (thousands of devices), modest retention, and a team that values simplicity over horizontal scale.

📌

B — Stream Pipeline + TSDB + Parquet Lake

Very high ingest, seconds-fresh dashboards, long-range analytics, tiered retention and multi-region resilience, as in this scenario.

📌

C — Single TSDB, Sync Rollups

Moderate scale in one region where the TSDB's own incremental rollups are enough and a durable replay log is not required.

📌

D — Cassandra Time Buckets

Massive write rates with mostly key-based reads (latest or recent readings per device) and multi-DC replication needs, paired with a separate analytics store.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using processing time instead of event time: Late or replayed data then lands in the wrong window. Use device event time with watermarks and an allowed-lateness policy.
  • Non-idempotent sinks: At-least-once delivery plus plain inserts double counts on retries. Upsert by (device, metric, window) or deduplicate on a natural key.
  • Rollups that cannot be re-aggregated: Storing averages loses information. Store count, sum, min and max so 1-minute rows can be merged into 1-hour rows.
  • Millions of tiny Parquet files: Writing one file per micro-batch ruins query performance. Roll files to a few hundred MB and compact hourly.
  • Ignoring the reconnect storm: After an outage every device flushes its buffer at once. Without Kafka headroom, consumer autoscaling and rate-limited backfill, the recovery causes a second outage.
  • Keeping raw data longer than policy: The requirement says full fidelity for 7 days. Raw data kept in S3 “just in case” costs money and may violate retention or privacy rules. Set explicit lifecycle expiry.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

IoT telemetry platforms usually evolve into a lambda-style layout with one source of truth, the log, rather than one database doing everything. A typical sequence:

  • Step 0 — Size it: Points/s, bytes per point, cardinality and burst factor (reconnect storms). Plan 3–5× headroom on the ingest path.
  • Step 1 — Ingest layer: An MQTT/HTTP gateway validates and authenticates, then produces to Kafka partitioned by device ID, using a schema registry (Avro/Protobuf) and recording device timestamps and receive timestamps separately.
  • Step 2 — Stream aggregation: Windowed rollups with watermarks and allowed lateness, writing to the hot store with idempotent upserts keyed by (device, metric, window start).
  • Step 3 — Hot store and lake: A TSDB holds 7 days of raw data plus recent rollups for dashboards. In parallel an archiver writes Parquet to S3 (hourly files, compacted to a healthy file size).
  • Step 4 — Batch downsampling and retention: Idempotent nightly jobs build 1-minute and 1-hour summary tables for the year of history. Lifecycle rules expire raw files on schedule.
  • Step 5 — Multi-region and observability: Mirror topics and replicate buckets, and monitor lag, late-event rate, small-file counts and dashboard query latency per region.

Keep the log as the source of truth and treat every derived store as rebuildable.

💡

Key Takeaway

For high-rate IoT metrics with long retention, use an append-only log as the backbone: stream processing for seconds-fresh aggregates, a hot TSDB for dashboards, Parquet in object storage for cheap history, and batch jobs for downsampling. It scales ingestion independently from queries, survives regional failures and keeps a year of data affordable.
