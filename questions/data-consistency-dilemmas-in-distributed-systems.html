Data Consistency Dilemmas in Distributed Systems

Sarah, a software engineer working for a rapidly expanding e-commerce application, is struggling to maintain data consistency among multiple microservices. Today every microservice writes straight to the database (in practice a shared one), so a single business action such as placing an order (create the order, reserve stock, charge the payment) can partially succeed and leave anomalies and mismatches. Some steps call external systems such as a payment gateway that cannot join a database transaction, and the business needs the services to stay available while the flow completes. She must choose the method that best restores consistency.

A
Deploy a two-phase commit protocol to guarantee transactional atomicity among the various services.
✗ Not Best
B
Add a message broker to facilitate asynchronous interactions between microservices, ensuring eventual consistency.
✗ Not Best
C
Route all write operations through a single, dedicated service that coordinates transactions and enforces strict consistency.
✗ Not Best
D
Rely on idempotent operations alongside compensating transactions to manage errors and preserve consistency without halting services.
✓ Best Answer
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — D: Idempotent Operations with Compensating Transactions (Saga)

The root problem is a business transaction that spans several services and can fail halfway. The pattern built for this is a saga: a sequence of local transactions, each paired with a compensating action that semantically undoes it, made safe to retry through idempotent operations. Failures are handled by retrying or compensating instead of by locking everything, so services stay available and the system converges to a consistent state. Here's why it stands out:

  • Correct failure semantics: Every step either completes or is compensated (refund the payment, release the stock, cancel the order), so the system never rests in a half-finished state. Intermediate states are explicit and visible (PENDING, CONFIRMED, CANCELLED).
  • Idempotency makes retries safe: Networks and brokers deliver at-least-once. With an idempotency key per operation, a duplicate has no additional effect, which turns unreliable delivery into reliable outcomes.
  • No distributed locks: Each service commits locally and stays available. Throughput is not capped by the slowest participant or by a coordinator.
  • Works with external systems: Payment gateways and third-party APIs cannot join a database transaction, but most offer idempotency keys and refund or cancel calls, which is exactly what the pattern needs.
  • Why not just add a broker (B)? A broker is the transport, not the correctness mechanism. Without idempotent consumers and compensations, at-least-once delivery creates duplicates and half-finished flows. Publishing an event after a database commit, without an outbox, also reintroduces the dual-write problem. Sagas normally run on a broker.
  • Well established: Sagas were introduced by Garcia-Molina and Salem in 1987. Today they run through orchestrators such as Temporal, Camunda or AWS Step Functions, or as choreography over Kafka or RabbitMQ with the transactional outbox pattern.

A production saga has an explicit state machine (orchestrated or choreographed), idempotency keys on every command and consumer, a transactional outbox for reliable publishing, timeouts and retries with backoff, idempotent compensations, and a dead-letter and reconciliation path. Also give each dataset one owning service, so no two services write the same tables.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🔒

A — Two-Phase Commit (2PC)

2PC gives atomicity, but participants hold locks while waiting for the coordinator, so a coordinator failure or a slow participant blocks the whole transaction. It lowers availability, couples services and their databases (all must support XA or similar), cannot include external APIs such as payment gateways, and scales poorly. Caveat: it can be acceptable inside one bounded context with a few XA-capable databases and low throughput, or where strict atomicity is non-negotiable.

📨

B — Message Broker with Eventual Consistency

A necessary building block but not sufficient alone. A broker decouples services, but without the outbox pattern a service can commit to its database and crash before publishing (or the reverse), and at-least-once delivery produces duplicates unless consumers are idempotent. It also does not define what happens when a later step fails; compensations do. In this scenario it is the transport underneath option D.

🚪

C — Single Write Service

Funnelling every write through one service removes concurrent-writer anomalies and centralizes rules, but it creates a bottleneck, a single point of failure and a distributed monolith in which every team depends on that service for every change. It also does not give cross-service atomicity when the work still spans external systems or several data stores. Caveat: single-writer ownership is the right idea per aggregate or data domain, not for the whole platform.

⚡

D — Idempotent Operations with Compensating Transactions (Saga) ✓

Rely on idempotent operations alongside compensating transactions to manage errors and preserve consistency without halting services.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Two-Phase Commit

Strict atomicity across a small number of XA-capable databases in one bounded context, at low throughput (for example moving a ledger entry between two internal databases).

📌

B — Message Broker with Eventual Consistency

Decoupling and fan-out where consumers tolerate eventual consistency (notifications, search indexing, analytics), always paired with an outbox and idempotent consumers.

📌

C — Single Write Service

A single data domain with strict invariants (inventory counts, account balances) where one owner service per aggregate enforces them.

📌

D — Idempotency + Compensation (Saga)

Multi-step business flows across services or external systems (orders, bookings, payments) that must stay available and converge to a consistent outcome.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • The dual write: Updating the database and then publishing an event as two separate steps. A crash in between loses the event or publishes one for a rolled-back change. Use the transactional outbox.
  • Non-idempotent consumers and compensations: Redelivery of a message then charges twice or refunds twice. Deduplicate on a message id with a unique constraint.
  • Assuming compensation equals rollback: Some effects cannot be undone. Order steps so the hardest-to-reverse step comes last, and design explicit business-level compensations.
  • Ignoring isolation anomalies: Other readers see PENDING data. Expose pending states deliberately and use semantic locks or versioning for contested resources.
  • No timeouts, dead-letter queue or reconciliation: Sagas silently stall when a service is down. Add timeouts, alerts and a repair path.
  • Idempotency keys that are not durable: Keys kept in memory or per instance are lost on restart. Store them durably, scoped to the operation, and enforce them with a unique constraint.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

Senior engineers combine several patterns, and they start by fixing data ownership:

  • Step 0 — Establish ownership: Each service owns its tables. No cross-service direct writes; other services use APIs or events.
  • Step 1 — Keep strong consistency inside a service: Use local ACID transactions within each service boundary.
  • Step 2 — Use sagas across services: Orchestration (Temporal, Step Functions, Camunda) for complex flows, choreography for simple ones.
  • Step 3 — Reliable messaging: Transactional outbox with change data capture (for example Debezium) or a polling publisher, plus an inbox or dedup table on consumers.
  • Step 4 — Idempotency everywhere: Idempotency keys on APIs, unique constraints and conditional updates.
  • Step 5 — Reconcile and observe: Reconciliation jobs, dead-letter queues and alerts for stuck sagas. Keep 2PC for the rare case that truly needs it.

Prefer local transactions plus sagas, and reserve distributed transactions for the narrow cases that justify them.

💡

Key Takeaway

Cross-service consistency comes from designing for failure: local transactions, idempotent retries and compensations, with a broker and an outbox as the plumbing. Give every dataset a single owning service, accept eventual consistency, and monitor for stuck or half-finished flows.
