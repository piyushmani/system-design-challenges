Oversell Prevention Throughput Concurrency
Challenge

Nora must prevent overselling without sacrificing throughput

Nora leads engineering for an e-commerce backend where Payments, Inventory, and Orders are separate microservices. During flash sales the platform must process tens of thousands of orders per minute while never overselling an item. The team also needs high availability during partial outages, low checkout latency, strong observability, and a well-defined failure-recovery path, without relying on convoluted distributed locks. Stock for popular SKUs is highly contended. She must choose the order-placement and inventory-reservation strategy that balances latency, consistency, and availability.

A
Use distributed ACID transactions (two-phase commit) across Inventory, Orders, and Payments, with serializable isolation on every checkout to guarantee no oversell.
✗ Not Best
B
Make Inventory the single authority for stock: all reservation logic lives in that service (sharded by SKU if needed), using local strict transactions for reserve, commit, and cancel, called synchronously by checkout, with asynchronous events driving later steps such as payment and fulfillment.
✓ Best Answer
C
Use the Saga pattern: each service commits its own local transaction and compensating actions undo earlier steps on failure, accepting eventual consistency across services.
✗ Not Best
D
Let each service run autonomously with optimistic concurrency control: version checks, conflict resolution, and client-side retries with backoff, with no central coordination.
✗ Not Best
✅

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — B: Inventory as the Single Source of Truth

Overselling is a violation of a single-entity invariant: available stock for a SKU must never drop below zero. Invariants like this are cheapest and safest to enforce where the data lives, in one place, with one local atomic operation. Making Inventory the authority turns a distributed-consistency problem into a local-transaction problem, and everything else (payment, fulfillment) can be asynchronous and compensable. Here's why it stands out:

  • Atomic invariant enforcement: A single conditional update (decrement only if available stock covers the quantity) or a serializable local transaction guarantees no oversell, with no cross-service locks and no coordinator.
  • Low latency: Checkout makes one synchronous call that performs one short local transaction. Nothing waits on payment, fulfillment, or other services to reserve stock.
  • Fault isolation and availability: A payment or fulfillment outage does not block reservations. The order stays pending and is completed or compensated through events. If Inventory itself is unavailable, checkout fails closed instead of overselling.
  • Clear failure recovery and observability: Reservations are time-limited holds with explicit states (reserved, confirmed, released, expired). Payment failure or timeout triggers a release, an expiry sweeper reclaims abandoned holds, and idempotency keys make retries safe. Reserved, confirmed, and released counts per SKU give direct visibility into correctness.
  • Scales with sharding: Because the invariant is per SKU, Inventory can shard by SKU. Extremely hot SKUs can be split into stock buckets or served by an atomic in-memory counter backed by a durable ledger.

Pair the reservation service with idempotency keys, reservation TTLs, outbox-published events, a reconciliation job, and dashboards for reservation latency, contention, expirations, and release failures. Load-test a single hot SKU with realistic flash-sale concurrency before launch.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🔒

A — Two-Phase Commit

2PC holds locks across services while a coordinator waits for every participant, so latency is bounded by the slowest service and a coordinator or participant failure can leave resources blocked. Under flash-sale contention this sharply reduces throughput and availability. It also couples teams and services tightly, and external payment gateways typically cannot participate in 2PC at all.

🔄

C — Saga Only

Sagas are the right tool for the multi-step order flow, but they provide no isolation: two concurrent checkouts can both see stock and proceed, and compensating after the fact means cancelling orders customers already saw confirmed. A saga still needs a strongly consistent reservation step to prevent overselling, which is exactly what option B provides. Complementary, but not sufficient on its own.

🔁

D — Optimistic Concurrency Everywhere

Optimistic concurrency works well when conflicts are rare. In a flash sale, many buyers contend for the same hot rows, so most attempts conflict, retry, and conflict again, causing retry storms and unpredictable latency. Version checks inside each service also do not coordinate stock across services, so there is still no single authority for the invariant.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Two-Phase Commit

A small number of tightly controlled databases from the same vendor, low contention, and a strong need for atomic multi-resource commit. Rare in microservice architectures.

📌

B — Inventory as Authority

A per-entity invariant (stock, seats, balances) under high contention, spanning services that cannot share a database.

📌

C — Saga Only

Long-running, multi-step business processes with independent steps and well-defined compensations, layered on top of a strongly consistent reservation step.

📌

D — Optimistic Concurrency Everywhere

Low-contention updates such as profile edits or document versions, where conflicts are rare and retries are cheap.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Read-then-write stock checks: Selecting the quantity and then updating it races under concurrency. Use one conditional statement that checks and decrements atomically.
  • No reservation expiry: Abandoned carts and crashed workers strand stock forever. Give every hold a TTL and a sweeper.
  • Non-idempotent reserve and release: Retries then double-decrement or double-release stock. Key reservations by order ID with a unique constraint and transition states only once.
  • Failing open when Inventory is slow: Proceeding without a confirmed reservation is how overselling happens. Fail closed, or offer a clearly labeled waitlist or backorder.
  • Holding a database lock across the payment call: Payment can take seconds. Reserve first in a short transaction, then charge asynchronously.
  • No reconciliation: Counters drift over time. Run jobs that compare reservations, confirmed sales, and available stock, and alert on any mismatch.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, teams protect the invariant first and then optimize throughput around it. A typical sequence looks like this:

  • Step 0 — Identify invariants: Decide which rules must never be violated (stock never negative, no double charge) and which can be eventually consistent.
  • Step 1 — Centralize stock ownership: Put reservation logic in Inventory with an atomic conditional update and an idempotency key per order.
  • Step 2 — Add the reservation lifecycle: TTL holds, confirm on payment success, release on failure or expiry, with events published through an outbox.
  • Step 3 — Orchestrate the rest with a saga: Payment, fulfillment, and compensations are handled by orchestration or choreography around the reservation.
  • Step 4 — Harden for hot SKUs: Stock buckets, in-memory counters with a durable ledger, and admission control such as a virtual waiting room.
  • Step 5 — Reconcile and rehearse: Compare ledger and counters regularly, alert on negative or drifting stock, and run game days that kill Payments or Inventory mid-sale.

Protect the invariant first, then optimize throughput around it.

💡

Key Takeaway

Enforce the no-oversell invariant in one place: the Inventory service, using a short local atomic reservation. Use time-limited reservations, idempotency keys, and asynchronous events with sagas for payment and fulfillment compensation. Avoid distributed transactions and uncoordinated optimistic concurrency, and engineer specifically for hot-SKU contention.
