Stop the Lock Step: Selecting a Deadlock-Aware Concurrency Strategy

Alex is a backend engineer reworking order processing for a fast-growing e-commerce platform. Each order creates accounting records in the Billing service and reserves stock in the Inventory service. The current design takes a simple distributed lock per request to serialize updates across services, which causes high latency, occasional system-wide stalls, and distributed deadlocks when transactions lock different accounts and SKUs in different orders. Alex needs an approach that eliminates deadlocks, keeps latency low, stays available during partial outages, and gives clear visibility into conflicts. Stock must never be oversold, billing records must stay accurate, and the business can accept a short-lived 'pending' order state while the workflow completes.

A
A central distributed lock manager: keep serializing multi-service updates through a replicated locking coordinator (such as Redlock on Redis or ZooKeeper) with deadlock detection, lock timeouts, and wait-time instrumentation, giving strict consistency but a serialized design.
✗ Not Best
B
Optimistic concurrency control with the Saga pattern: remove global locks in favor of per-entity versioning (row version or compare-and-set) with idempotent operations and fast retries, use a Saga with compensating actions for cross-service consistency, and monitor traces and conflict/retry metrics.
✓ Best Answer
C
Distributed transactions with Two-Phase Commit: a transaction coordinator drives a single atomic commit across Billing and Inventory, holding locks at both participants to provide strict ACID guarantees.
✗ Not Best
D
Eventual consistency with commutative updates (mergeable events or CRDTs): let the services process updates concurrently and resolve conflicts through background reconciliation or CRDT merges, accepting an eventually consistent model.
✗ Not Best

Answer & In-Depth Explanation

🏆 Best Answer — B: Optimistic Concurrency + Saga

Distributed deadlocks come from holding locks while waiting for other locks across service boundaries. The cure is to stop holding locks across services at all. Option B does that: each service protects its own invariant with a short, local, conditional write (compare-and-set or a guarded decrement), conflicts become fast retries instead of waits, and the cross-service workflow is a Saga in which every step is idempotent and has a compensating action. Nothing blocks on anything else, so there is nothing to deadlock, and a failure in one service only delays or compensates its own step. Here's why it stands out:

  • Deadlocks are structurally eliminated: Deadlock requires waiting while holding. With optimistic writes there is no waiting; a conflicting writer fails the compare-and-set and retries. Any remaining local row locks inside a single database should be taken in a consistent global order (for example, SKUs sorted by ID).
  • Latency scales with the work, not the queue: There is no lock queue in front of each order. A reservation is a single conditional update, typically sub-millisecond to a few milliseconds in the owning database, and orders for different SKUs proceed fully in parallel.
  • Oversell is prevented by the invariant, not by the lock: A guarded update such as available = available - q WHERE available >= q is atomic in the Inventory database, so two orders cannot both take the last unit.
  • Availability during partial outages: If Billing is slow or down, the saga pauses at that step (the order stays pending) or compensates by releasing the reservation, while Inventory keeps serving other orders. No coordinator or lock service is a hard dependency for every request.
  • Clear conflict visibility: Version conflicts, retry counts, saga step durations, and compensation counts are first-class metrics, and a saga log gives every order an inspectable state history.
  • Proven pattern: Sagas were introduced by Garcia-Molina and Salem (SIGMOD 1987) for long-lived transactions and are now standard in microservice order flows; optimistic concurrency with compare-and-set underlies most modern NoSQL stores and is widely used with SQL.

A production saga has a persisted state machine per order, idempotency keys on every step, reservations with TTLs, bounded retries with jittered backoff, compensations that retry until success, and dashboards for conflict rate, saga duration, and compensation counts. If conflict rates on a few hot rows dominate, introduce per-key serialization for those keys rather than going back to global locks.

🔍 Why Not the Other Options?

Each alternative is a valid technique — but none addresses the core problem as directly:

🔐

A — Central Distributed Lock Manager

A lock manager keeps the serialization that causes the latency: every order waits on the coordinator, and deadlock detection merely resolves deadlocks after they have already stalled work. It also adds a critical dependency, and lock-based mutual exclusion in distributed systems is subtle (Martin Kleppmann's analysis of Redlock argues that without fencing tokens, a paused client holding an expired lock can corrupt data). Caveat: for a rare, genuinely exclusive operation (a nightly migration, a singleton job), a lock with a fencing token is appropriate; it just should not sit on the hot path of every order.

🤝

C — Two-Phase Commit (2PC)

2PC gives atomicity but holds locks at every participant for the entire protocol, including at least one network round trip, so latency rises and contention grows. It is a blocking protocol: if the coordinator fails between prepare and commit, participants stay locked until it recovers. It does not remove deadlocks either, since each participant still takes local locks in whatever order requests arrive. Caveat: within a single database or a tightly coupled pair of resources on reliable infrastructure, 2PC can be acceptable, but it is a poor fit for independently owned microservices.

♾️

D — Eventual Consistency + CRDTs

Commutative merges are great for counters, sets, and presence, but stock reservation has a hard invariant (never below zero) and billing needs exact, auditable totals. Plain CRDT counters let concurrent decrements both succeed, which oversells inventory, and background reconciliation after the fact means customers may be charged for goods that do not exist. Caveat: bounded counters or escrow-style reservations (each region owns a slice of stock) can enforce limits with limited coordination, but that is a purpose-built design rather than generic 'eventual consistency'.

🎯 When Would You Pick Each?

Every option is the right answer in a specific context:

📌

A — Central Lock Manager

Rare, genuinely exclusive operations (leader election, migrations, singleton jobs) with fencing tokens; not a per-request hot path.

📌

B — OCC + Saga

Multi-service business workflows (orders, bookings, payments) where each service owns its data, contention is moderate, and short-lived intermediate states are acceptable.

📌

C — Two-Phase Commit

A small number of tightly coupled resources on reliable infrastructure that truly need atomic commit, often inside one database or a transaction manager you control.

📌

D — Eventual + CRDTs

Naturally mergeable data (counters without limits, sets, collaborative state, presence) where invariants are weak or enforced by bounded designs.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Read-modify-write without a precondition: Reading stock, computing in the app, and writing back races with other writers. Use a conditional update or a version check so stale writes fail.
  • Unbounded optimistic retries: Retry loops without limits or jitter turn contention into a storm. Cap attempts, add jitter, and fail with a clear error.
  • Non-idempotent saga steps: Redelivered commands double-reserve or double-bill. Use idempotency keys per order and step.
  • Reservations that never expire: A crashed workflow strands stock. Give reservations a TTL and a reaper, and make confirmation idempotent.
  • Compensating by deleting: Removing a ledger row destroys the audit trail. Post a reversal entry instead.
  • Wrong step order: Charging the customer before reserving stock forces refunds on every stock-out. Put the hardest-to-undo step last.

🌍 Real-World Best Practice

In practice, teams shrink the scope of coordination step by step:

  • Step 0 — Draw the lock graph: Find which operations hold which locks and in what order. Wait-time metrics and traces usually point at a few hot rows or inconsistent lock order.
  • Step 1 — Fix lock ordering locally: Within each database, acquire row locks in a consistent order (for example, sorted by SKU) and keep transactions short, which often removes most deadlocks quickly.
  • Step 2 — Use guarded atomic writes: Replace read-then-lock-then-write with conditional updates that enforce invariants in a single statement.
  • Step 3 — Replace the cross-service lock with a Saga: Persist order state, make each step idempotent, and define compensations.
  • Step 4 — Handle hot keys: Shard counters or serialize hot SKUs through a per-key queue.
  • Step 5 — Observe conflicts: Track compare-and-set failure rate, retries per request, saga duration, and compensation rate, and alert on drift.

Keep invariants enforced where the data lives; coordinate across services with workflows, not locks.

💡

Key Takeaway

Deadlocks vanish when nothing waits while holding a lock across services. Enforce each invariant locally with conditional, versioned writes, and coordinate across Billing and Inventory with a Saga of idempotent steps and compensations. Accept visible intermediate states, design for hot keys, and keep locks only for rare, truly exclusive operations.
